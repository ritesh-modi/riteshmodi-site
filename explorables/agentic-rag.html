The pipeline that is allowed to try again

A question arrives as one slip of text. Ordinary retrieval searches once and hands whatever it found to the model. Agentic retrieval lets the model look at what came back, decide it is not enough, and go round again. Below, that loop is running.

The slip moves on its own. Click any station to hold it there.

A model answers from what is in front of it, and from nothing else

Answer this, quickly: what is the refund window on order 88214?

You cannot. Not because you are slow, but because nothing in front of you says. You would need the order record, and the refund policy, and you have neither. Notice that you knew instantly which two things you were missing. Hold onto that, because it is the whole job of the machinery in this piece.

A language model is in the same position, with one difference: it cannot go and look. Everything it is allowed to use has to be sitting in one block of text that gets handed to it at the moment it runs. That block is called the context window, and it is finite. Text goes in, an answer comes out, and the window is emptied again.

Think of the window as a tray with a fixed number of slots. Each slot holds one slip: a short piece of text, of any origin. Your question is a slip. A paragraph from a policy document is a slip. A result handed back by some other system is a slip. They are all the same kind of object once they are on the tray, and the model cannot tell which of them came from where unless the text itself says so.

Picture it A locum doctor covering a shift they have never worked. They are competent, they trained for years, and they have never met this patient. Everything they can act on is whatever is clipped to the board at the end of the bed. Put the allergy note on the board and they will avoid the drug. Leave it in a filing cabinet in another building and they will not, and they will not know they are missing it. The board is not the doctor's memory. It is the doctor's entire world for the next ten minutes.

With the question alone, the model has to guess, and it will. With the order record and the policy both loaded, the answer is arithmetic. Nothing about the model changed between those two states. Only the tray changed.

Everything that follows is machinery for deciding what goes on the tray.

Which raises the obvious question: the policy document exists, it is sitting in a wiki somewhere, so who put it on the tray? In the figure it was you, clicking. In production nobody is clicking.

What it memorised during training is not the same as what your company wrote down

Ask a model when the French Revolution started and it will tell you 1789 with no help at all. Ask it your own refund window and it will also answer, in the same confident register, and the answer will be invented.

Both answers come out of the same place: the statistical residue of everything the model read during training, baked into its weights. Call it parametric memory, because it lives in the parameters. It is genuinely large and genuinely useful, and it has two properties that matter here. It is frozen at the moment training stopped. And it never contained your internal documents in the first place.

The failure this produces is not a blank. A blank would be easy to catch. The model produces a fluent, plausible, specifically-worded policy that no one at your company ever wrote, because producing plausible text is exactly what it was built to do.

Picture it Somebody who has read ten thousand company handbooks and never yours. Ask them what your notice period probably is and they will say three months, because three months is what handbooks usually say. They are not lying and they are not guessing wildly. They are answering from the average of everything they have seen. The answer is well calibrated to a population and wrong about you, and it arrives sounding exactly as certain as the right one would.

Figure 2. Switch the question. The model is identical in both cases. Only whether the answer was ever in the training data changes.

So the fix is not a better model. A better model has the same hole, further away. The fix is to get the right text onto the tray before the model runs.

Which means something has to find it.

Retrieval is a paste job that happens before the model wakes up

Here is the trick, and it is less clever than its reputation. Before the model runs, a separate piece of software searches your documents, takes the few paragraphs that look most relevant, and pastes them onto the tray next to the question. Then the model runs, once, and answers from the tray. That is retrieval. The model is not involved in the search and does not know it happened.

The documents are cut into paragraph-sized pieces first. Each piece is a passage, and once it is on the tray it is just another slip. The whole collection of passages, prepared and searchable, is the index.

Searching it by keyword would work badly, because the question and the passage rarely use the same words. So each passage is converted into a list of a few hundred numbers, its embedding, positioned so that passages about the same thing land near each other regardless of wording. The question gets converted the same way. Nearness between the question's numbers and a passage's numbers is the similarity score, and it is a number between zero and one.

Why nearness rather than word overlap: it is the only measure here that survives a reader asking about "money back" when the document says "refund". The two phrases share no words and land in almost the same place.

The search returns the highest-scoring few. How many is a setting, written top-k, where k is simply the count. Take k of them, paste, run the model.

Picture it A clerk at a counter who cannot read the question and does not understand the filing system, but is very fast and has a rule: fetch the three folders whose spines look most like the request slip, put them on the desk, walk away. They never check whether the folders actually answer anything. The clerk optimises for looking relevant, which is nearly always the same as being relevant, and the gap between nearly and always is where the rest of this piece lives.

Figure 3. Pick a question, then raise or lower k. Every passage is scored; the top k are pasted onto the tray. Scores are cosine similarity, computed by this page from the small vectors shown.

For a question whose answer sits in one paragraph, this is the end of the story. It works, it is cheap, it runs in a couple of hundred milliseconds, and most of what gets called RAG in production is exactly this and nothing more.

Now ask it something whose answer is not in any single paragraph.

One search cannot answer a question whose halves live in different documents

Try this one: is order 88214 still inside its refund window?

Say what you would have to do. First find the order and read its date. Then find the policy and read the number of days. Then subtract. Three moves, and the second one is impossible until the first is done, because until you have read the order you do not know it was a gift purchase, and gift purchases have their own rule.

A question like this is multi-hop: the answer is assembled from facts that sit in different places, and knowing which place to look second depends on what you found first.

Now watch what single-shot retrieval does with it. The question goes in as one lump of text, so its embedding is an average of everything it mentions: part order record, part refund policy, squarely on neither. The three passages that score highest are a different customer's order at 0.876, the right order at 0.830, and the standard refund rule at 0.816. One of those three is genuinely needed. The gift rule, which is the one the right order says applies, scores 0.711 and misses the cut. The model, which cannot tell that anything is missing, answers from the three it got.

Picture it Sending one runner to the archive with a note reading "the Henderson matter and whatever the rule is about those". They come back with three folders that all mention Henderson. Nobody brought the rule book, because the note never named it, because naming it required reading a folder first. The runner did their job perfectly and the desk is still missing half of what it needs, and no one at the desk can tell.

Figure 4. Toggle between what was needed and what was fetched. The two sets overlap on one passage. The missing half is never requested, so nothing reports it missing.

The failure has a specific shape worth naming. Nothing errored. No component behaved incorrectly. The retriever returned its best matches, and they were its best matches. The model answered from its tray, and it did. The system has no place where "I do not have enough to answer this" could be noticed, because no part of it ever compares what came back against what was asked.

Add one step that reads the results before answering, and the pipeline stops being a pipeline.

That step needs to be able to do something about what it finds. Which means it needs to be able to send the runner back.

Let the model read its own results, and the line becomes a loop

The change is small to describe and large in consequence. Instead of pasting the passages straight onto the tray and answering, put a decision in between: show the model what came back and ask it one question before it is allowed to answer. Is this enough?

If yes, answer. If no, it may write a new search and go round again. A model given that authority, over a set of actions it may choose between, is what the word agent means here. Nothing mystical: a model whose output is read as a decision about what to do next rather than as prose for a human.

The code that reads that decision and dispatches it is the controller. It is ordinary software, usually a loop with a counter, and it is the piece that actually holds the system together. Each pass through it is one iteration.

Below is the whole architecture with one request actually moving through it. The graphic stays; the text moves. Watch the dashed leg from station 6 back to station 3, because that leg is the only thing separating this diagram from the pipeline that failed.

Figure 5. One request, traced. Rust is the question travelling out, green is graded context coming back, dark is the answer leaving. A link only carries colour while something is on it. Click any station to hold there, or scroll to walk them in order.

1 · Intake. The question arrives and becomes a slip, alongside anything the session already carries: who is asking, what they asked two turns ago, which account they are in. This is the only station a human touches.

2 · Router. A cheap first decision: does this need the documents at all? "What is your VAT number" does not. Sending everything to retrieval is the most common and most expensive design mistake here, because retrieval is the slow part and most traffic does not need it.

3 · Query writing. The question as typed is usually a bad search query. This station rewrites it, and on later turns it writes a different query, informed by what the last turn failed to find. That rewriting is where multi-hop questions get taken apart.

4 · Retrieval. The search from before, unchanged: embed the query, score the index, return the top k passages. Nothing here knows it is inside a loop.

5 · Grader. The new part. Each returned passage is judged against the question: does this actually help answer it? Passages that do not are dropped rather than pasted. This is what stops the tray filling with plausible irrelevance.

6 · Decide. The controller compares what survived grading against what the question needs. Enough, and it moves on. Not enough, and it sends control back to station 3 with a note about what is still missing. This arrow backwards is the only structural difference between this diagram and the pipeline from before.

7 · Synthesise. Everything that survived is on the tray. The model writes the answer, and is instructed to cite which slip each claim came from, which is only possible because the slips are still distinct objects at this point.

8 · Answer. Out to the caller, with citations and a record of the path taken. That record is not decoration: when this system is wrong, the path is the only way anyone finds out where.

Picture it A returns desk with a supervisor. The runner brings folders; the supervisor reads them before the clerk is allowed to answer, and can say "this is the wrong Henderson, go back and ask for the gift purchase rule". The runner is not smarter than before. The desk simply stopped treating the first delivery as final.

Two stations deserve their names. Grading is judging a returned passage against the question and dropping it if it does not help. Routing is deciding, before any of this runs, whether it needs to run at all.

The loop is now capable of going round forever, and something has to stop it.

One number decides how many times it may go round

The controller holds a counter. Before it sends control back to the query writer it checks that counter against a ceiling, and if the ceiling is reached it stops looping and answers with whatever it has. That ceiling is the iteration budget, and it is a single integer in a config file.

It is also the entire difference between the two architectures in this piece. Set it to zero and the loop never runs: retrieve once, answer, which is exactly the pipeline that failed the two-part question. Raise it and the same code becomes agentic. There is no other switch.

At zero it fails, and fails silently, with a fluent answer built from one correct passage and one irrelevant one. At one it notices the gap but runs out of turns before it can use what it noticed. At two it gets there. At three, four and five it also gets there, having spent more to arrive at the same place.

Picture it Telling the runner how many trips they are allowed before the desk has to answer regardless. One trip is the old system. Two is usually the whole gain. Ten trips does not buy a better answer, it buys nine identical answers and one very long wait.

Almost everything agentic retrieval is credited with comes from the second turn existing at all.

The turns after that are not free, and the bill arrives in two currencies at once.

Every extra turn is paid for twice, in seconds and in tokens

A turn is not one model call. It is a query rewrite, a search, and a grading judgement on each returned passage, and the grading is itself a model call per passage. Set k to five and one turn costs you six model calls, not one.

Those calls are sequential, because turn three's query depends on turn two's result. They cannot be run in parallel, so their latencies add. On the figures below, a single-shot pipeline answering in 400 milliseconds becomes a three-turn agent answering in 2.8 seconds, and a six-turn one takes 6.4. Somewhere in there is the point where a person watching a cursor blink decides the thing is broken.

Figure 7. The same budget as Figure 6, and the two stay in step. Drag it: quality stops climbing once the budget reaches two, and the two costs do not.

The shape of those three lines is the argument for setting the budget low and leaving it there. Quality is a step, not a ramp: it moves once, between zero turns and two, and then flattens. Latency and spend are close to linear and keep going. Anywhere past the flattening point you are buying nothing at a steady price.

Picture it A taxi meter that runs the whole journey while the useful part of the trip finished at the second junction. Nothing is going wrong. You are simply still paying.

All of which assumes the grader is right about what it drops. It is a model, and it is not always right.

The grader is a model too, and its mistakes are the expensive ones

Every station added above is a model making a judgement, which means every station is a place where a wrong judgement can be made confidently. Three of those go wrong often enough to design around, and they fail in different directions.

Pick one and watch where it breaks the path.

Figure 8. Choose a failure. The station where it originates turns red, and the consequence is traced downstream from it.

The first is a grader that passes a passage it should have dropped. The passage is on topic and does not answer the question, which is the hardest case to judge and the most common thing an index returns. Everything downstream now treats it as evidence, and the citation on the final answer points at a real document that does not say what the answer claims. This is worse than the original failure, because it now comes with a footnote.

The second is a loop that will not settle. The query writer rewrites, the retriever returns much the same passages, the grader drops them again, and the controller sends it back. It terminates only because the budget runs out, and then it answers anyway, from the same thin material it had on turn one, having spent five turns to get there.

The third is not a wrong answer at all. One question arrives that the router should have handled without retrieval, takes five turns and thirty model calls, and a load spike does the same to every request behind it. Nothing in the traces looks wrong. The bill and the latency graph both look wrong.

Picture it The supervisor at the returns desk waving through a folder that mentions Henderson twice and settles nothing. The desk is now more confident than it was before the supervisor existed, and it is confident about the wrong thing.

None of these are solved. They are bounded, and bounding them is what the production version is mostly made of.

In production the loop runs inside limits it is not allowed to exceed

A deployed version of this is the diagram from before with a ring of constraints around it, and the constraints are unglamorous. Every one of them exists because something in the previous section happened to somebody.

Figure 9. Click a layer to see what it caps and which failure it is there for.

A hard ceiling on turns, on total model calls, and on wall-clock time, whichever is hit first, because a budget expressed only in turns does not bound a turn that is itself slow. A cache in front of the router, because a surprising share of production traffic is the same question, and a cached answer costs nothing and takes no turns. A fallback path that returns the single-shot answer with a note saying the search was inconclusive, because that is more useful than a confident wrong one and far more useful than a timeout. And the trace, stored, because when this system is wrong the only way anyone finds out where is by reading the path the request actually took.

Picture it A kitchen where the chef may re-fire a dish, twice, and the pass has a timer on it. The freedom to try again is real and it is fenced, and the fence is the part that ships.

The loop is the idea. The fence is the product.

Underneath the vocabulary, the whole of it is one arrow drawn backwards from the grader to the query writer, and a counter to stop it running forever.

Into the weeds

Chunking is upstream of all of it. Passages are cut before anything runs, and a cut through the middle of a table or a procedure produces a passage that is unanswerable no matter how many turns you allow. A large share of what looks like retrieval failure is a cutting decision made weeks earlier.

Grading can be cheaper than a model call. A reranker scores query and passage together in a single small model pass, an order of magnitude below a language model call. Most production graders are a reranker with a threshold, and only ambiguous cases go to the expensive judge.

The budget does not have to be static. Some controllers raise it when the router flags a question as multi-hop and cap it at one otherwise, which gets most of the quality for a fraction of the average cost. It also gives you a new way to be wrong, in the router.
