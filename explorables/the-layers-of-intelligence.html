eight words, four of which are rings

Four of these words nest.The rest are notlayers at all.

AI, machine learning, neural networks and deep learning really do sit inside one another, like India, Maharashtra, Mumbai, Bandra. Then the diagram keeps going — and everything after that point is a different kind of word entirely. Almost every version of this picture you have seen gets that part wrong.

The question with an annoying answer

People keep asking some version of: is ChatGPT AI, or is it machine learning, or is it GenAI? The honest answer is unsatisfying and completely correct: yes, all of them, at the same time.

That is not a dodge. It is the same kind of answer as this one:

“Do you live in India, or in Maharashtra, or in Mumbai, or in Bandra?”

Nobody finds that confusing. All four are true at once. They are not four different places you might live; they are four levels of zoom on one place. You would use different ones in different conversations — “India” to someone abroad, “Bandra” to someone across town — and switching between them is not a contradiction.

Two things fall straight out of that, and both are worth stating plainly:

The bigger the ring, the older, broader and vaguer it is. “India” covers enormous ground and tells you very little about any particular person. Same with “AI”.

The smaller the ring, the newer, narrower and more specific. “Flat 4B” is extremely informative and applies to almost nobody. Same with “ChatGPT”.

the question that saves you moneyIf a vendor tells you their product “uses AI”, how much have they actually told you? About as much as telling you they live in India. The useful question is always which ring.

The four that genuinely nest

These four are settled and agreed on. Notice, in the drawing at the top, that the dates go down as the rings get smaller. That is not a coincidence — each inner ring is a later, more specific answer to the problem the outer ring set.

Artificial intelligence — the ambition

Any machine doing something that would count as intelligent if a person did it. The useful analogy is transport. Transport is not a technology; it is a goal — moving things from A to B. Bullock carts, bicycles, ships, aircraft and lifts are all transport, and they share almost no engineering. “How does transport work?” is not a well-formed question.

AI is exactly like that. It is a goal, not a technique, which is why the outer ring is so wide and so vague, and why the term has meant completely different things across seventy years.

The example that proves this ring is bigger than learning: IBM’s Deep Blue beat world champion Garry Kasparov in 1997. Unquestionably AI, and a landmark. It contained no machine learning whatsoever — hand-written evaluation rules plus enormous brute-force search. Nothing about it improved on its own.

AI does not require learning. Most people assume it does. It doesn’t, and the counter-example is famous.

Machine learning — the method that won

The part of AI where the rule is learned from examples instead of written by a human. If AI is “transport”, machine learning is “engines” — one particular approach that turned out to work so well it now dominates. Plenty of transport has no engine, and there was a great deal of transport before engines existed.

Now the correction that matters most, because it is the opposite of what the press implies. The techniques living in this ring — logistic regression, decision trees, random forests, k-means, nearest neighbours — were almost all invented between the 1950s and the 1990s. None of them is a neural network. None is deep learning. And they still run most of the world’s actual production machine learning: credit scoring, churn prediction, demand forecasting, insurance pricing, fraud, medical risk scores.

There is a solid practical reason. On tabular data — rows and columns, the kind of data most organisations actually have, with headers like customer_id and last_order_value — a well-tuned random forest routinely matches or beats a deep neural network. It trains in minutes instead of days, on a laptop instead of a cluster, on thousands of rows instead of millions, and it comes with an explanation you can show a regulator.

If your problem is a spreadsheet, you probably do not need deep learning — and you certainly should not start there. Deep learning earns its keep on images, audio, video and language: data with no natural columns.

Neural networks — one family of technique

A way of learning that stacks layers of very simple units, loosely inspired by neurons. The honest analogy is an assembly line of extremely dim workers. No individual worker understands the product. Each does one trivial thing — “is this edge dark or light?” — and passes it on. The complexity comes entirely from how many of them there are and how they are wired, not from any one being clever.

That is the real picture. A single unit does something so simple you could do it on paper in about four seconds. Millions of them, arranged in layers, produce ChatGPT.

The historical point is worth making, because it corrects a false impression of inevitability. The idea dates to 1958. It then failed, repeatedly, for fifty years. Through the 1980s and 1990s it was a fringe technique that mostly lost to the methods in the ring above. Two full “AI winters” — periods when funding collapsed because the field had over-promised — happened with neural networks sitting right there, not working.

So what changed? Not the idea. The idea was already there. Data and computing power arrived. That is a genuinely useful lesson for judging any technology today: the same idea can be worthless and then transformative without changing at all.

Deep learning — the same thing, with many more layers

Neural networks with a large number of layers, which turns out to change what is possible, not merely how well it works. And here is the single most important technical idea on this page:

Classical machine learning needs a human to decide what to look at. Deep learning works out what to look at, by itself.

Where the drawing breaks

Up to deep learning, every word is the same kind of word: a category of technique, each a genuine subset of the one before. After deep learning, the words are different kinds of things entirely, and stacking them in one picture makes them look like a hierarchy when they are not.

Cricket fixes this instantly. Consider: sport → ball sports → cricket → T20 → the IPL → Mumbai Indians → the match on Saturday.

The first three are a clean nesting: cricket really is a kind of ball sport, which really is a kind of sport. Then it changes. T20 is a format — a set of rules. The IPL is a tournament that uses that format. Mumbai Indians is a team that plays in it. Saturday’s match is an event.

Is Mumbai Indians “inside” T20? Loosely, in a way you would accept in conversation. But it is not the same kind of thing at all, and drawing it as one more nested circle implies something false — that every T20 match involves Mumbai Indians, which is nonsense.

The famous diagram does exactly this. It takes an architecture, a capability, a class of model, a brand, a version and a product, and draws all six as though they were nested categories.

The distinction that costs real money

A trained model is an engine. A chat product is a car. When you type into a chat product you are getting the model plus a great deal of surrounding machinery — a chat interface, conversation memory, a hidden system instruction shaping its behaviour, safety filters, and the ability to search the web or run code.

When you buy access through an API instead, you get closer to the bare engine and you build the rest yourself. Three consequences follow:

Behaviour differs. The same underlying model can answer differently in the product versus the API, because the product wraps it in instructions you never see.

You are usually buying a product, not a model. Comparing one chat product against another mixes categories — you are comparing two wrappers, and the wrapper often matters more than the model for everyday use.

What your company builds sits at the model layer. The interface, guardrails and integrations are yours to design, which is where most of the actual value and most of the actual risk lives.

Why the ring is a budget decision

Beginners assume the rings are trivia. They are not — they are a cost and risk map. Which ring your problem sits in determines almost everything about how you would resource it.

Three things worth saying out loud from that table.

The foundation-model column has no build cost and no end to its running cost. That is a genuinely different economic shape from anything most organisations have bought before, and budgets built for one-off software projects handle it badly.

The explainability row is why banking and insurance still live in the second ring. That is not conservatism. If you must justify a declined loan to a regulator, an unreadable model is not an option.

Being in the outer, older ring is not a defeat. A simple model that ships this quarter and can be explained beats a deep learning project that lands next year and cannot.

What this map does not tell you

Two honest limits, because a diagram this tidy invites overconfidence.

It shows techniques, not capability. New inner circles have appeared every few years and will keep appearing. Nothing in this picture tells you how far the field will go, and any slide claiming otherwise is selling something.

The leaf names drift constantly. Particular model families and version numbers change on a timescale of months. Treat the specific product names anywhere on this page as illustrative and check a live source before quoting current capabilities or rankings. The shape is stable; the labels at the edges are not.

One more thing worth heading off. “Should we move from machine learning to AI?” is a sentence that gets said in real meetings, and it does not mean anything — nobody moves from ML to AI, because they were already doing AI. Usually the sentence means “we should use large language models”, which is a specific and expensive choice that deserves to be argued on its merits rather than smuggled in through vocabulary.

Four sentences to keep. These are not competing things; they are nested things, like India, Maharashtra, Mumbai, Bandra. AI does not require learning, and learning does not require deep learning. Most working machine learning in business is in the second ring, not the fourth — and that is a feature, not a shortfall. After deep learning the words stop being layers: they become blueprints, jobs, brands and products, and every confusing diagram you will ever see is caused by drawing those four as circles.
