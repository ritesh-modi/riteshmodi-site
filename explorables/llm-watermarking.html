Text provenance

How watermarks are used with LLMs

Nothing in a sentence records who wrote it. A watermark puts that record inside the words themselves, by quietly biasing which words get picked. Below is a sentence a model wrote. The shading was always there.

Click the sentence to hold the light on it, or let it come and go.

A watermark is a mark that lives inside the thing, not on top of it

Find a banknote. Hold it up to a window.

A face appears in the blank part of the paper, and it was not printed there. During manufacture the wet pulp was pressed thinner in that shape, so less material sits in those places and more light comes through. The mark is not ink sitting on the surface. It is a variation in the paper itself, made at the moment the paper was made.

That is a watermark: a signal hidden inside an object, put there while the object was being made, invisible in ordinary use, and readable by anyone who knows how to look. Papermakers have used them since the thirteenth century, first as a workshop's signature and later as the thing that makes a forged note hard to pass.

Three properties are doing the work, and every one of them will come back later in this piece, so they are worth naming now. The mark is imperceptible in normal use: you handle money all day without seeing it. It is inseparable from the object: you cannot peel it off, because it is not a layer, it is the paper. And it is verifiable: hold it to the light and the answer is plain.

Picture it A baker who presses a small initial into the base of every loaf before it goes in the oven. You will never notice it while eating. You cannot scrape it off, because it is not icing, it is the shape of the bread. But turn the loaf over and it is unmistakable, and no other bakery makes that mark. The signature survives because it was made at the same moment as the thing it signs, out of the same material.

Text has none of this by default. A paragraph is just characters. There is no pulp to press and no surface to emboss.

So the question this piece answers is what you would press into a sentence, given that a sentence has no thickness. And before that, a blunter question: why bother at all?

Guessing from style fails in the one case that matters

Read these two sentences and decide which was written by a machine.

1. The library closes at six, so we should probably head over now.
2. Delve into the multifaceted landscape of contemporary librarianship.

Most people pick the second, and the reasoning is sound: it is bland, it is padded, it uses words that turn up more often in generated text than in speech. That reasoning is exactly what an AI text classifier does. A classifier is a program that reads a passage and outputs a probability that a machine wrote it, trained by showing it many examples of each kind and letting it learn what separates them.

It works, in the sense that it is better than a coin. It fails in the way that matters.

The failure has a name. A false positive is the case where the detector says "machine" about text a person actually wrote. Non-native English writers use simpler constructions and a narrower vocabulary, which is precisely the signature these tools were trained to flag, and so their essays get flagged at a much higher rate than native writers' essays do. In 2023 OpenAI withdrew its own AI Text Classifier for exactly this reason, citing a low rate of accuracy. Meanwhile the fix in the other direction is trivial: ask the model to write less blandly, or paste the output through a rewriter, and the style signature that the classifier depends on is gone.

Notice what has gone wrong. The detector is reading the surface of the text and guessing backwards to its origin. Surfaces can be imitated in both directions. A careful human can look machine-like by accident; a machine can be told to look human on purpose.

Picture it Trying to work out which of two paintings is the forgery by standing back and judging the brushwork. Sometimes you are right. But a good forger studies brushwork, and a nervous original artist can paint stiffly on the day. What a gallery actually wants is not a better eye, it is a record made by the artist at the moment of painting, which the forger was never in a position to make.

Figure 2. Score each passage. The classifier only ever sees the words. Two of these four judgements are wrong, and the two it gets wrong are the two that cost somebody something.

A guess made after the fact is not evidence. A record made during the act is.

That reframes the problem. Instead of inspecting finished text, put the mark in while the text is being produced, in the one place the producer controls and the imitator does not.

To do that you need to know what a model is actually doing while it writes, because that is where the mark has to go.

Every word a model writes is a draw from a bag, not a decision

Finish this out loud: "the cat sat ___".

You probably said "on", possibly "down", maybe "quietly". You did not consider "photosynthesis". Several words were live options, some more likely than others, and one of them came out.

A language model does the same thing, and does it in a way you can watch. It has a fixed list of pieces it is allowed to write, called its vocabulary. Each piece is a token: usually a word or a fragment of one. Real vocabularies run to about a hundred thousand tokens; this page uses four, so the arithmetic stays small enough to do by hand.

At every position the model produces a number for each token in the vocabulary saying how well that token would continue the text so far. Turned into percentages that add up to 100, those are the odds for the next token. For "the cat sat", the four-word toy vocabulary might come out as: on 40, down 30, quietly 20, up 10.

Then comes the step that matters most here, and it is the one people usually assume works the other way. The model does not take the highest number. It samples: it draws one token at random, with each token's chance of being drawn equal to its share of the odds. Forty percent of the time you get "on". Ten percent of the time you get "up". If it always took the top token, the same prompt would give the same answer every time, and the writing would be noticeably flat and repetitive.

Why this matters for everything that follows: sampling means there is slack. At most positions several tokens are defensible, and which one arrives is genuinely a matter of chance. That slack is empty space nobody is using. It is exactly where a watermark can be hidden.

Picture it A cloth bag holding a hundred marbles. Forty are labelled "on", thirty "down", twenty "quietly", ten "up". You do not choose a marble, you pull one out without looking, and the counts decide how often each label comes up. Refill the bag with different counts for the next word, and pull again. Nothing about the sentence forces one specific marble, so the hand that fills the bag has room to lean, and no reader can tell.

Figure 3. Draw from the bag, once or a hundred times. The tally underneath is what actually came out, so it wanders around the odds rather than matching them exactly.

So the model is already rolling dice several hundred times per page, and nobody is watching the dice. The trick is to make the rolls slightly crooked in a pattern only one party can reproduce.

Which requires a secret.

A secret number, mixed with the words already written

Here is the constraint the whole design has to satisfy. The bias has to be reproducible by the model's owner, so they can check for it later. It has to be unpredictable to everyone else, so they cannot strip it out. And it has to change at every position, or the same words would be favoured all through the text and a reader would notice the tic.

Three ingredients meet all three conditions.

The first is the key: a large number chosen once and kept secret by whoever runs the model, say 8,675,309. It never changes and it is never published. It is the only thing a checker needs and the only thing an attacker lacks.

The second is the look-back: the words immediately before the position being written. Turn them into a number by any fixed recipe, as long as it is the same recipe every time. Adding up letter values works: a is 1, b is 2, and so on. "sat" becomes 19 + 1 + 20 = 40. The look-back is what makes the bias move, because the preceding words are different at every position in the text.

The third is a hash: any fixed rule that takes numbers in and gives a scrambled number out, where a small change to the input gives a completely different output, and where you cannot run the rule backwards to recover the inputs. Multiplying by the key and taking a remainder is a toy version of one. Real systems use a proper cryptographic hash, but the useful property is the same: the output looks random, yet the same inputs always produce the same output.

Feed the look-back and the key into the hash and out comes a number that is unpredictable unless you hold the key, and identical every time for the same preceding words. That is the whole trick. A private, repeatable coin toss, available at every position in the text.

Picture it Two people agree a rule before parting: take today's newspaper headline, add up the letters, multiply by a number only they know, and the answer says which cafe to meet at. Anyone watching sees them meet somewhere different each day with no pattern. Anyone who knows the number can work out the cafe in advance every single time. The unpredictability comes from the changing headline, and the reproducibility comes from the shared number, and you need both or the scheme is either predictable or uncheckable.

Figure 4. Change the preceding word, then change the key. Same word and same key always give the same scrambled number. Change either and it jumps somewhere unrelated. Every number here is computed by this page.

The number that falls out is not yet a watermark. It is raw material. Something has to turn one number into a bias over a hundred thousand tokens.

The scrambled number cuts the dictionary in half

Take the number that came out of the hash and use it to sort every token in the vocabulary into one of two piles. Any rule works as long as it is fixed and it depends on that number. In this page's toy, tokens are numbered 1 to 4, the scrambled number is added to each, and the ones that come out even go in the first pile.

The first pile is the favoured list: the tokens that are about to get a small advantage. The second is everything else. In the original research these are called the green list and the red list, which is worth knowing because you will meet those names anywhere else you read about this, and they mean exactly the two piles here. The share of the vocabulary that goes green is written gamma, and it is usually one half.

Two things about this split are easy to skim past and both are load-bearing.

It is arbitrary. There is nothing about "on" that makes it a greener word than "down". Which pile a token lands in is decided by a scrambled number, and it carries no meaning at all. That is the point: a meaningful split would be visible as a style.

And it is redrawn at every position. The look-back changes with each word written, so the hash changes, so the split changes. A token that was favoured after "sat" is very likely unfavoured after "down". Over a paragraph, every token spends roughly half its time in each pile, which is why no reader can point to a word the text is fond of.

Picture it A teacher who, before each round of a game, deals every child a red or blue card at random, then says the blue cards get a small head start this round. Next round the cards are dealt again. No child is favoured overall, no child can predict their colour, and yet in any single round the advantage is real and the teacher knows exactly who had it. The dealing is reshuffled constantly so that nothing accumulates in the open, while the teacher's record of each deal stays perfectly recoverable.

Figure 5. Step through the preceding words. Green is the favoured list, rust is not. Watch which side each word lands on as the look-back changes. Nothing about the word decides it.

Two piles, redrawn constantly, known only to the key holder. Now the favoured pile has to be turned into an actual advantage without wrecking the sentence.

Add a little to the favoured, rescale, and draw as normal

The bias itself is one line of arithmetic. Take the odds the model produced. Add a fixed amount, called delta, to every token on the favoured list. The numbers no longer add up to 100, so rescale them: divide each by the new total and multiply by 100. Then sample exactly as before.

That is the entire mechanism. Everything else in this piece is either setting it up or measuring it afterwards.

Follow it once, all the way through, with the toy vocabulary and a key of 7. The steps are pinned on the left while the arithmetic scrolls past.

Figure 6. One word, start to finish. Click any step to jump to it.

Look back. The text so far ends "the cat sat". Take the last word, "sat", and add up its letters: s is 19, a is 1, t is 20. That gives 40. Any fixed recipe would do; this one is just easy to check by hand.

Mix in the key. The key is 7. Multiply: 40 times 7 is 280. Now scramble it by taking the remainder after dividing by 13. Thirteen goes into 280 twenty-one times, which is 273, leaving 7. The scrambled number is 7.

Cut the vocabulary. Number the tokens: on is 1, down is 2, quietly is 3, up is 4. Add 7 to each and keep the even results. On gives 8 and quietly gives 10, so both are favoured. Down gives 9 and up gives 11, so neither is.

Load the odds. The model's honest odds were on 40, down 30, quietly 20, up 10. Add a delta of 10 to the two favoured tokens: on becomes 50 and quietly becomes 30. The four now total 120, so divide each by 1.2 to get back to a hundred: on 42, down 25, quietly 25, up 8.

Draw. Lay the four out along a line from 0 to 100. On holds 0 to 42, down holds 42 to 67, quietly holds 67 to 92, up holds the rest. Pick a number at random: 63. That lands in down. The sentence becomes "the cat sat down".

And notice what just happened. Down was not on the favoured list, and down is what got written. The bias is a nudge, not a rule. Any single word proves nothing, which is exactly why the text still reads naturally, and exactly why detection has to count across hundreds of words rather than inspect one.

Now write the next word and watch the ground move. The look-back is no longer "sat", it is "down": 4 plus 15 plus 23 plus 14 is 56. Times 7 is 392. The remainder after dividing by 13 is 2. Adding 2 and keeping evens favours down and up this time, the exact opposite pair.

That reshuffle is what keeps the watermark invisible. If "on" and "quietly" were favoured all the way down the page, the text would read like someone with a verbal tic. Instead the favoured pair changes at every position, driven by words that are themselves changing.

Picture it A croupier with a very slightly weighted wheel, who moves the weight to a different pocket before every spin according to a schedule in their pocket. No player watching a hundred spins can find the bias, because it never sits still. The house can prove it was there, because the house has the schedule and can check every spin against it afterwards.

One word tells you nothing. Three hundred words tell you almost everything.

Delta was set to 10 above with no justification. It is the only number in the scheme a designer really chooses, and it decides how much of the text you have to read before the mark shows up.

One number decides how hard the thumb presses on the scale

Delta is a dial with nothing clever behind it. It is the amount added to every favoured token before rescaling, and the designer picks it. Larger means a stronger signal in the text and a weaker sentence. Smaller means the opposite. There is no setting that avoids the trade, which is why it is worth feeling rather than reading about.

Hold the same odds and the same favoured pair steady, and move only delta.

Figure 7. Drag the strength. Green bars are the favoured pair, rust the rest. Every percentage is recomputed by this page from odds of 40, 30, 20, 10.

At zero the odds are untouched: on 40, down 30, quietly 20, up 10. The favoured pair already holds 60 percent of the probability, because "on" happened to be the likeliest word anyway. There is no watermark here and nothing to find.

At 10 the numbers become on 42, down 25, quietly 25, up 8, and the favoured pair now holds 67 percent. That seven point gap is the entire signal. A reader would need to compare against the unwatermarked odds to notice anything, and they do not have those.

At 50 the favoured pair holds 80 percent, and the damage starts to show: "quietly" has been pushed from 20 percent to 35, so the model now writes "the cat sat quietly" far more often than the language warrants. The text is getting stranger in a way that is hard to point at but easy to feel.

At 1000 the favoured pair holds 98 percent. The model is effectively forbidden from using half its vocabulary at every position, and the writing collapses into something forced. Detection would be trivial and nobody would use the output.

Picture it Salting a stew so that the cook can later prove it was theirs. A pinch is undetectable to the diner and provable by a lab. A spoonful is provable by anyone and ruins the dish. There is no amount of salt that is both instantly obvious and completely tasteless, and pretending otherwise is how you end up with an inedible stew.

Every watermark is a quality budget spent on provability.

Real systems sit near the bottom of that dial. In the published research the boost is applied to the model's raw scores before they are turned into percentages, and a value of about 2 there is common, which works out to a gentler nudge than the +10 on percentages used here. Percentages were easier to do in your head, and the shape of the trade is identical.

A nudge that small raises an obvious objection. If the text barely changes, how would anyone ever prove the mark is there?

The checker replays the same arithmetic and keeps score

Detection needs no access to the model, no record of what it generated, and no copy of the original prompt. It needs the key and the suspect text, and it works like this.

Start at the second word. Take the word before it, run the same look-back and hash with the same key, and rebuild the favoured list for that position. Then ask one question: was the word actually written in that list? Write down yes or no. Move to the next word and repeat to the end.

What comes out is a hit rate: the fraction of positions where the written word turned out to be favoured. This is the whole of detection. There is no model involved, only bookkeeping.

Now the arithmetic that makes it work. If nobody watermarked the text, the favoured list at each position is an arbitrary half of the vocabulary that has nothing to do with what the writer chose, so the written word falls in it about half the time. Human writing, other models' writing, text watermarked with a different key: all of them land near 50 percent. Not because anything about them is random, but because the list they are being scored against is.

If the text was watermarked with this key at a delta of 10, the odds were tilted toward the favoured list at every position, and the hit rate comes out nearer 65 or 70 percent.

Neither number is impressive on its own. The gap between them is the evidence, and it only becomes evidence once you have counted enough positions.

Picture it A card game where, before each hand, a sealed envelope names a suit. Players never see the envelopes. Afterwards a referee opens all three hundred and counts how often the card played matched the named suit. An honest table matches about a quarter of the time. A table being fed cards matches far more. No single hand looks wrong, and the referee never needs to have watched the game, only to hold the envelopes.

Sixty-seven out of a hundred against an expected fifty. That looks convincing, but "looks convincing" is not a standard anybody should accept when the output might get a student expelled.

Sixty-seven out of a hundred is suggestive; two hundred out of three hundred is proof

Toss a fair coin ten times. Seven heads would not surprise you. Toss it three hundred times and get two hundred heads, and something is wrong with the coin. Same bias, wildly different strength of evidence, and the only thing that changed was how long you watched.

Statistics puts a number on that. Start from what the world looks like if there is no watermark, which is called the null: every position is an independent coin toss with a half chance of landing in the favoured list. Across 300 positions the null expects 150 hits. It will not deliver exactly 150, and the typical wobble around that expectation has a size you can compute: for 300 tosses at even odds it is about 8.7 hits.

Now measure how far the observed count sits from the expectation, counted in wobbles. That distance is the z-score. A z-score of 1 means the result is one ordinary wobble away, which happens constantly. A z of 4 means four wobbles away, which essentially does not happen by chance.

Watermarked text at a hit rate of 67 percent gives 201 hits out of 300. That is 51 hits above expectation, and 51 divided by 8.7 is a z of about 5.9. Unwatermarked text sits at a z near 0. The gap is not a matter of opinion.

The practical consequence is that detection strength depends on length, not just on delta. The same watermark that is unprovable in a tweet is overwhelming in an essay, because the expectation grows with the number of positions while the wobble grows only with its square root. This is why every honest description of these systems states a minimum text length, and why a two-sentence quote cannot be tested at all.

Picture it Judging whether a die is loaded. Three sixes in a row is a story you would tell at dinner. Sixty sixes in three hundred rolls, against the fifty you would expect, is a die you would take away from the table. The bias never changed between those two cases. Only the number of rolls did, and that is the entire reason short text cannot be tested.

So a long enough passage settles it. That holds right up until somebody changes the words.

Three ordinary situations, and the watermark survives one of them

Take three cases that would actually arrive at a university office in the same week.

Maya submits 900 words on the causes of the First World War. She generated it, then spent twenty minutes fixing the bits she disagreed with and rewriting the opening paragraph in her own voice. Roughly 15 percent of the words changed. The other 85 percent are still sitting in their original positions with their original look-backs, so they still hit their favoured lists. The count falls from about 603 to about 520 out of 900, and the z-score falls from around 10 to around 4.5. Still far outside chance. The watermark holds.

Daniel pastes the same essay through a free rewriting tool and submits what comes out. Every sentence has been restated. Almost no word sits after the word it originally followed, so almost every look-back is different, so almost every favoured list is a fresh arbitrary half of the vocabulary that has nothing to do with what is written. The hit rate collapses to roughly 50 percent and the z-score to under 1. Published measurements put detection rates below 0.3 after a single pass of language-model paraphrasing. This is a paraphrase attack, it costs nothing, and it works.

Priya submits a 40-word short answer. It was generated and untouched. At 40 positions the null expects 20 hits with a wobble of about 3.2, and a watermarked hit rate of 67 percent gives 27. That is a z of about 2.2, which sounds like something and is not enough to accuse anyone of anything. There is no watermark strength that fixes this, because the problem is the sample size.

A fourth case is worth naming because it catches people out. Watermarking needs the model to have had a genuine choice. Ask for the boiling point of water at sea level, or for a function that reverses a string, and there is only one sensible continuation at nearly every position. The amount of real choice available is called entropy, and where entropy is low the model cannot be nudged without producing a wrong answer, so no mark gets embedded. Short factual replies and most code are close to unmarkable.

Picture it A watermark in paper survives being folded, posted and photocopied badly. It does not survive being retyped. Paraphrasing is retyping. The mark was never in the meaning, it was in the specific sequence of words, so anything that keeps the meaning and replaces the sequence takes the mark with it.

So this is not a solved problem and nobody serious claims it is. It is a tool that answers one question well, and the question is narrower than most people assume: was this specific run of words produced by this specific model, in a passage long enough to count, that nobody has rewritten.

Which raises the question of who is running it anyway, given all that.

It is already running, on a scale that surprises people

Google DeepMind published SynthID-Text in Nature in October 2024, and by the time the paper appeared the system had already been operating inside Gemini for months, which made it the first statistical text watermark deployed to consumers at scale.

Its mechanism differs from the green list in one interesting way. Instead of adding a bias to the odds and sampling once, it runs what the authors call tournament sampling: several candidate tokens are drawn, then paired off in rounds like a knockout bracket, and at each round the winner is the candidate scoring higher against a key-derived function. The survivor gets written. The effect is a similar tilt toward key-favoured tokens, arrived at by a different route.

The quality question was answered with numbers rather than assurances. The team compared feedback across close to 20 million Gemini responses and found no significant difference in thumbs-up and thumbs-down rates between watermarked and unwatermarked output. That is the strongest available evidence that a well-tuned delta really is invisible to readers, and it is worth more than any argument from first principles.

The regulatory push is now ahead of the technology. Transparency duties under the EU AI Act became applicable on 2 August 2026, and Article 50 requires providers to mark synthetic content in a machine-readable way. Text is the hardest case in that rule, for the reason this piece has already shown: a paragraph can be retyped or lightly rewritten and the mark goes with it. The associated guidance settles on a layered approach, combining metadata attached to the file, a statistical watermark inside the content, and server-side logging of what was generated. None of the three is sufficient alone, and each covers a way the others fail.

Picture it A parcel with a delivery label on the outside, a stamped serial inside the box, and an entry in the courier's own database. Peel the label and two records remain. Nobody builds it that way because any one method is weak; they build it that way because the failure modes do not overlap.

The watermark is not proof of authorship. It is one line of evidence that happens to be very hard to forge and very easy to destroy.

Which is the honest place to leave it: useful for tracing your own model's output at scale, unreliable for accusing an individual, and worthless against anyone who spends thirty seconds trying to remove it.

Into the weeds

Gamma does not have to be one half. The share of the vocabulary that goes green is a second dial. A smaller green list makes each hit more surprising, so the z-score climbs faster per word, but it also constrains the model harder at every position. One half is the common default because it maximises the information per token without narrowing the choice more than necessary.

The look-back can be longer than one word. Hashing the previous four tokens instead of one makes the favoured list harder to guess and the scheme harder to break by changing a single word. It also makes it more fragile to editing, because changing one word now disturbs the four positions that follow it rather than one. Real schemes trade these against each other explicitly.

Detection has a false-positive rate you can actually set. Because the null is a known distribution rather than a trained model's hunch, you can pick a z threshold that corresponds to a stated error rate, say one in a million, and report it. This is the concrete advantage over style classifiers, which cannot tell you what their false-positive rate is on a population they were not trained on. It does not make the answer certain, it makes the uncertainty quotable.

A watermark is not encryption and hides no message. It carries roughly one bit: this text was, or was not, produced under this key. Multi-bit variants exist that can encode a user identifier, and they need considerably more text to read out.
