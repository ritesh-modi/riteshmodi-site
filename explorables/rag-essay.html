five documents · one question

RAG, FROM THE GROUND UP

RAG, from the ground up

Your questions are answered by a language model. Your documents are not inside it. So before it can answer about your refund policy, something has to find the right passage and hand it over.

This essay builds that something from the beginning: the old keyword search, the new meaning search, how the two are combined, and how to measure whether it works. The keyword scores are computed from the five sample articles. The vector map is a hand-placed toy, and the model sizes and cost figures are real or labelled as examples. You do not need to know any code to follow it.

SCROLL

1 · THE PROBLEM

Five articles, one question

A customer asks: “How do I get my money back?” The answer is in our help centre, in an article called Getting your money back. But the question does not use the article’s words. Nothing in the question says “refund”.

Our corpus is five short articles: a refund policy, shipping, a warranty, the money-back article, and a sizing guide. The probe runs down them. It is looking for the one that answers the question, and it finds the money-back article, D4.

1 · THE GAP

A model answers from memory

A large language model learned from a huge amount of public text. It knows that most shops refund within some window, and it can write a sensible-sounding paragraph about it. It has never seen our article.

The grey dots under the articles are what the model cannot see. Asked directly, it will guess. A guess about your refund policy is the most dangerous kind of wrong answer, because it sounds right. The fix is not a bigger model. It is to give the model the right page at the moment it answers.

1 · THE GOAL

Retrieve first, then answer

Retrieval-augmented generation, or RAG, is that fix. It has two steps. A search step finds the few passages most likely to answer. The model then writes its answer from those passages, and can point at them.

The gold flows show the idea. Only the two articles that answer the question travel to the model. The other three stay behind on the shelf.

in

a question, and a store of documents the model has never seen

out

an answer built from the passages the search found

means

the model’s job is to read, not to remember. Search decides what it reads.

where a RAG answer fails

DEEP DIVE · ACT ONE

Three places a RAG answer can go wrong

A wrong answer comes from one of three stages. Retrieval missed the right passage. Context put it in the prompt badly. Generation ignored it or invented something. Each has a different fix, so find the stage first.

RETRIEVAL MISS

If the answer is not retrieved, nothing downstream can save it

Thirty candidate passages, and the right one sits at rank 17. Searching the top five never sees it. Widening the window to 20 or more finds it. This is a recall problem, so the fixes are in chunking, hybrid search and query rewriting, not in the prompt.

A reranker only reorders what was retrieved. If the answer is outside the candidates, no reranker can help.

the inverted index

ACT TWO

Keyword search

The oldest good idea in search: make a list of which documents contain each word, then look the word up.

2 · THE INDEX

Words point at documents

Break every article into words, lowercase them, and build a table. For each word, write down the documents that contain it. That table is the inverted index. It is “inverted” because the article points to its words; the index points from each word back to its articles.

Now search for refund. Look up the word, and follow its line to document one. Done in a fraction of a millisecond here, and it stays fast as the collection grows.

2 · THE MISS

The same meaning, different words

Now search for money back. The index finds the money-back article, because it contains both words. But the search never looked at the word refund for this question, so it never looked at a document that matters just as much to a customer.

Keyword search does exactly what it is told. It cannot see that “money back” and “refund” mean nearly the same thing. That gap is what the next acts close.

2 · THE LIMIT

Exact words, or nothing

One word finds one article. Two words, joined by OR, find both. The index can do this very quickly, but every match must be a literal match.

Keyword search is precise about words and blind to meaning. It is the right tool when the customer uses the exact product name, and the wrong tool when they describe their problem.

in

a word, or several joined by AND and OR

out

the list of documents that contain those words

means

every hit is a real word match; no hit can mean something else

postings · AND and OR

DEEP DIVE · ACT TWO

The index is a grid you can read

Rows are words, columns are documents, and a dot is a posting: this word appears in this document. Each row is the postings list for one word. The whole index is this grid, stored sparsely.

BOOLEAN QUERIES

OR adds columns; AND keeps only the shared ones

item OR refund   = {D1, D4} ∪ {D1} = {D1, D4}
item AND refund  = {D1, D4} ∩ {D1} = {D1}

The engine reads only the rows the query names, then combines them. That is why a query with many OR terms is slower, and why AND can return nothing when no document has every word.

What to expect. OR returns every document containing either word. AND returns only documents with both. With refund and money, the OR result is D1 and D4, and the AND result is empty: no article has both words.

BM25 · the score for one word

ACT THREE

BM25: counting words with judgement

Matching words is not enough. Some matches matter more than others. BM25, the standard keyword ranking in search engines, scores each match by three simple judgements.

The first is about repeats. A word that appears in a document earns points, but the first mention adds a lot and the fifth adds very little. This is called saturation, and it stops a document from scoring high just by repeating a word. The curve on the stage is the score for one word, using the usual setting for its strength, k1 = 1.2.

3 · RARE WORDS

Rare words are worth more

The word the appears in nearly every article, so it tells you nothing. The word refund appears in one, so it tells you a lot. BM25 calls this the inverse document frequency, and gives each word a weight from how many documents contain it.

On our five articles, refund, policy and money each appear in one article, so each is worth 1.39. Back, item and receipt appear in two, so each is worth 0.88.

3 · LENGTH

A long article gets a fair comparison

A long article is more likely to contain any word by chance. BM25 compares each document’s length with the average length, and discounts long ones a little. Our articles average 68.6 tokens, counting each title. The dashed line is that average.

This is the b = 0.75 setting. Set it to zero and length stops mattering. Set it to one and length matters as much as it can.

3 · THE RANKING

One score per document, per query

Add the scores for each query word and you get one number per document. For refund policy, the refund article scores 3.16 and nothing else matches. For money back, the money-back article scores 3.09, and the refund article keeps 0.83 for the word back. The bars move between the two as you scroll.

BM25 counts words. It does not know that “money back” means “refund”. It is fast, explainable and strong on exact terms, which is why it is still the default in most search engines.

in

the query words, and the words in each document

out

one score per document; sort them, highest first

means

higher is more word-level evidence, with rare words and first mentions counting most

BM25 · term by term

DEEP DIVE · ACT THREE

BM25, one term at a time

A document’s score is the sum of one contribution per query word. Here are the two contributions for D1 and the query “refund policy”.

score(D, Q) = Σ IDF(w) · f(w,D)·(k1+1) / ( f(w,D) + k1·(1 − b + b·|D|/avgdl) )

IDF(w) = ln( 1 + (N − n(w) + 0.5) / (n(w) + 0.5) )

refund: f = 2, IDF 1.386, contribution 1.843. policy: f = 1, IDF 1.386, contribution 1.320. Total 3.16.

k1 · REPEATS

k1 decides how quickly repeats stop counting

At k1 = 0 a second mention adds nothing. At k1 = 2 the curve keeps rising. Most engines start at 1.2. Tune it on your labelled queries, because the right value depends on how long and repetitive your documents are.

b · LENGTH

b decides how much a long document is punished

At b = 0 length is ignored. At b = 1 the score is scaled fully by length against the average. Our 68-token warranty article scores higher than the 77-token refund article for the same single word once b is near 1. Choose b by testing, not by habit.

Improving BM25 means tuning k1 and b on your queries and boosting titles. Changing the formula itself rarely helps.

  2. Open Lab 3 · Score one document, word by word. Keep the query refund policy and the document D1.
  3. Drag k1 to 0, then 2. Drag b to 0, then 1.

What to expect. At k1 = 1.2 and b = 0.75, D1 scores 3.16: refund about 1.84 and policy about 1.32. At k1 = 0, repeats add nothing. At b = 1, the shorter document gains against the longer one.

vectors · meaning as position

ACT FOUR

Vectors: meaning as numbers

Keyword search cannot tell that two different words mean the same thing. A vector can. It turns a piece of text into a list of numbers, and places that text at a point in a space.

Imagine a flat map. The horizontal axis is “how much it is about money”. The vertical axis is “how much it is about delivery”. Each article is a dot, placed by two numbers.

The refund article and the money-back article sit far to the right and low down. The shipping article sits high on the delivery axis. Their positions are their meaning, as far as this map is concerned.

This map is two-dimensional so you can see it. Real models place each piece of text on a map with hundreds of axes.

4 · DIMENSIONS

A dimension is one number in the list

Each axis on the map is one dimension, and each dimension is one number in the vector. Our map used two. Real embedding models use many more. The grid on the stage is one sentence turned into 384 numbers, the size of a small, fast model.

More dimensions can hold more detail, but they also take more memory and more time to compare. Size is a trade-off, not a score.

4 · NEARBY MEANS SIMILAR

The question lands next to its answer

Turn the question into a vector, put it on the same map, and look at what is near it. The query refund lands next to the refund article. The query money back lands next to the money-back article. Neither word was matched; both were placed.

Vector search is this: put everything on one map, then find the nearest points to the question. The next act says how “nearest” is measured.

in

a piece of text, turned into a list of numbers by an embedding model

out

a point in space, with as many coordinates as the model has dimensions

means

texts with similar meaning get similar numbers, whatever words they use

embeddings · what dimensions cost

DEEP DIVE · ACT FOUR

Dimensions are paid for on every chunk

An embedding model turns a text into d numbers. The memory for an index is the number of chunks times d times the bytes per number. A 32-bit float is four bytes.

storage bytes = N · d · 4

1,000,000 chunks × 384 dims  ≈ 1.5 GB
1,000,000 chunks × 1536 dims ≈ 6.1 GB
1,000,000 chunks × 3072 dims ≈ 12.3 GB

More dimensions cost memory and search time, and they do not guarantee better answers. Measure recall on your own questions before paying for them.

cosine · the angle between two meanings

ACT FIVE

Cosine similarity: the angle, not the length

Two vectors are close when they point the same way. The standard way to measure that is cosine similarity: the cosine of the angle between them.

5 · WHY THE ANGLE

Length does not change the answer

Stretch the second arrow and its position changes, but the angle does not. The cosine is the same at any length. That is useful: a long article and a short one about the same thing point the same way.

The scale: 1 means the same direction, 0 means unrelated. Our refund vector and refund article give 0.998. Where “close enough” starts depends on the model and on your data.

Cosine ignores how big a vector is. It only sees direction. That is why it is the default for text.

5 · READING IT

A match and a miss, side by side

The gauges show the same question against two articles on our toy map. The refund article reads 0.998. The shipping article reads 0.359. The gauge sweeps as you scroll, so you can see how far the value travels.

There is no fixed threshold that means “relevant”. What counts as close depends on the model and on your data. You learn the cut-off by measuring, in the last acts.

5 · RANKING BY COSINE

Sort the documents by how close they point

Compute the cosine between the question and every document, then sort from highest to lowest. The bars are that list. The refund article and the money-back article are at the top, the shipping article at the bottom, and the warranty and sizing articles sit in between.

in

two vectors of the same length

out

one number between −1 and 1

means

near 1: same direction, close in meaning. Near 0: unrelated. Length is ignored.

cosine · a dot product over lengths

DEEP DIVE · ACT FIVE

Cosine is a dot product divided by two lengths

cos(a, b) = (a · b) / (‖a‖ · ‖b‖)

a = (0.88, 0.18)    b = (0.82, 0.22)
a · b  = 0.7216 + 0.0396 = 0.761
‖a‖·‖b‖ = 0.898 × 0.849  = 0.762
cos    = 0.761 / 0.762   = 0.998

The dot product rewards size and agreement. Dividing by the lengths removes size, which leaves only direction.

SCALE

Length cancels out

Multiply a by any positive number s. The dot product grows by s and so does ‖a‖. Their ratio is unchanged. Two passages of different length about the same thing get the same cosine.

If you normalise every vector to length 1 at indexing time, the dot product is the cosine. Search faster, same ranking.

  2. Open Lab 5 · Angle, not length. Set a to point at the question, then b toward a different document.
  3. Scale a by 2, then by 3, and read the cosine each time.

What to expect. The cosine changes only when the directions change. Scaling a moves the dot and the length together, so the cosine stays the same.

vector search · brute force, then the models

ACT SIX

Vector search and the embedding model

Now the whole process. Something has to turn text into vectors. That something is an embedding model: a neural network trained so that texts with similar meaning get similar numbers.

The question and the documents go through the same model. This matters. If the question and the documents are turned into numbers by different models, the numbers mean different things and cannot be compared.

6 · NEAREST NEIGHBOURS

Grow a ring until it touches the answers

With every document as a point, the search is a simple question: which points are nearest to the query point? The ring grows from the query until it reaches the two closest documents: the money-back article and the refund article.

The simplest method is to measure the distance to every document and keep the closest. That is called brute force. It is exact, and it is fine for thousands of documents. For millions, search engines use an index for vectors, such as HNSW, which finds nearly the same neighbours much faster.

6 · THE SCAN

Compare the question with everything

Brute force, drawn as bars. On our toy map, the query money back gets a cosine score against each document. The money-back article scores 0.999, the refund article 0.997, and the shipping article 0.481.

Notice what happened to the word “refund”. The query never used it. The meaning was enough to bring the refund article to the top.

6 · WHICH MODEL TO USE

Choose the embedding model by testing, not by size

Three real models, three sizes: a small one with 384 numbers per text, and two from OpenAI with 1,536 and 3,072. More numbers can capture more detail, but they cost more storage and slow each comparison.

Choose on four things. Language: does the model understand your language and your product words? Size: how much storage and speed can you afford? Price: does it run on your servers or someone else’s, and what does each request cost? Quality: how often does it bring back the right document for your questions? The last one is the only one that decides. Test it.

in

a text, and a model that was trained to place texts by meaning

out

a vector; compare vectors with cosine; keep the nearest

means

the same model must be used for documents and questions. More dimensions is not automatically better.

exact versus approximate search

DEEP DIVE · ACT SIX

Exact search reads everything

Brute force computes the distance from the question to every point. For N points of d dimensions that is about N × d multiply-adds per query. On a million chunks at 1,536 dimensions, that is about 1.5 billion operations for one question.

APPROXIMATE · HNSW

A graph walk visits a few points

An approximate index links each point to a few close neighbours. A query starts at one point and hops to whichever neighbour is closer to the question, until no hop helps. It visits a handful of points and usually lands on the true nearest, or one very near it.

M               links per point       more → better recall, bigger index
efConstruction  effort to build       more → better graph, slower build
efSearch        candidates per query  more → better recall, slower query

ANN recall = matches with brute force / brute-force matches

Measure ANN recall against brute force on a sample of questions before you trust the speed.

RAG · the pipeline

ACT SEVEN

RAG, end to end

The pieces are now ready. Put them in order. A question goes to a retriever, which returns the best passages. Those passages are placed into a prompt with the question. The language model reads the prompt and writes the answer.

7 · WHAT THE MODEL SEES

The model sees the prompt, and nothing else

The index is not visible to the model. Neither are the other 28 manuals. The model sees only the text in its prompt: our instructions, the passages the retriever chose, and the question.

This is the most important fact about RAG. If the right passage is not in the prompt, the model cannot use it. If a wrong passage is in the prompt, the model may use that instead. The quality of the retrieval sets the ceiling for the answer.

7 · THE CONTEXT WINDOW

The model can only read so much at once

Every model has a context window: the most text it can read in one request, measured in tokens. A token is a piece of a word, roughly three quarters of an English word on average.

Five help articles fit with room to spare. Thirty manuals do not. So RAG does not paste everything in. It picks the few passages that matter. The window is the reason retrieval exists at all.

7 · PROS AND CONS

What RAG buys you, and what it costs

RAG is the usual first choice for answering from your own documents, because it is quick to set up and easy to change. The benefits are real, and so are the costs: retrieval can miss, there are more parts to look after, and every question costs an extra search.

When a RAG answer is wrong, the question to ask is “did the right passage reach the model?” before “is the model bad?”.

7 · THE COMPARISON

Three ways to answer the same question

The model alone guesses. Keyword search alone finds the refund article and misses the money-back one. RAG, with a meaning search in front, finds the right article, reads it, and says where the answer came from.

in

a question, a retriever, and a model that reads only the prompt

out

means

context budget · lost in the middle

DEEP DIVE · ACT SEVEN

The window is a budget, not a bucket

passage budget = window − instructions − question − reserved output
passages that fit ≈ passage budget / tokens per passage

With an 8,000-token window, 400 for instructions, 30 for the question and 600 kept for the answer, about 23 passages of 300 tokens fit. Fill it further and something must be cut, silently or by design.

ORDER

Put the strongest passage at the edges

Research on long contexts (Liu and colleagues, “Lost in the Middle”, 2023) found that models use the start and end of a long input more reliably than its middle. The curve here is illustrative, not a measurement. Put the best passage first, and keep the total short enough that the middle matters less.

chunking · cutting documents to fit

ACT EIGHT

Chunking: cutting documents to a useful size

So far each document has been a single unit. Real documents are long: a manual can run to thousands of words, and a whole manual is too big to place on the map as one point, or to fit in a prompt.

The answer is to cut each document into chunks, and to search the chunks instead. Each chunk gets its own vector and its own entry in the index.

8 · FIXED SIZE

The simplest method: cut every N words

Fixed-size chunking cuts every 25 words, regardless of what the words say. It is easy, fast and predictable. Its weakness is visible on the stage: the rust marks show cuts that land mid-sentence, so a chunk can start with half a thought.

8 · OVERLAP

Repeat the edges so no idea is lost

Overlap makes each chunk repeat the end of the one before. Here 6 words are shared, about a quarter of each chunk. An idea that straddles a cut now appears whole in at least one chunk.

The cost is storage and repetition: the same words are embedded twice, and results can repeat. A common starting point is 10 to 20 percent. Treat it as a setting to test.

8 · SENTENCE-AWARE

Cut at the full stops instead

Sentence-aware chunking moves each cut to the nearest sentence end. The chunks are slightly uneven in size, but each one starts and ends on a complete thought. Other methods go further and follow headings, or split by meaning. The more a method respects the document’s own structure, the more work it takes to set up.

8 · SIZE

Small chunks are precise. Large chunks have context.

A 5-word chunk matches a question exactly, but it may not contain enough to answer it. A 75-word chunk, the whole article, has the context, but it mixes several points, so its vector is blurred. Around 25 words suits a short article like this one; real documents usually use a few hundred tokens. It is not a rule.

in

a document, a target size, and some overlap

out

chunks, each with its own vector and its own index entry

means

what is searched is the chunk, so the chunk is what you must get right. Measure with the tests in the last acts.

chunking · counts and methods

DEEP DIVE · ACT EIGHT

How many chunks a document becomes

n = ⌈ (T − o) / (s − o) ⌉

Hypothetical: T = 10,000 tokens, o = 50, s = 500
n = ⌈ 9,950 / 450 ⌉ = 23 chunks

Smaller chunks mean more chunks, more index entries, more storage, and more duplicates from overlap. Watch the count move as the size changes.

METHODS

Where the cut goes depends on the method

Fixed size cuts at a count of tokens and can split a sentence. Recursive prefers paragraph breaks, then sentences, then words, until a chunk fits; it is the usual default. Sentence-aware cuts at full stops. Structure-aware follows headings and keeps tables whole. Semantic cuts where the meaning of neighbouring sentences shifts, which costs an embedding per sentence. Parent–child searches small chunks and returns their parent section.

Choose by recall@k on your labelled questions. The method that finds the answer wins, whatever it looks like.

hybrid search · one question, two lists, one answer

ACT NINE

Understanding the query, then searching well

Search quality is decided before any document is looked at. The question has to be understood, and the words used for the search have to be chosen with care.

The customer’s sentence is not the best search. First find the intent: a refund request. Then rewrite the query into words the documents use, such as “refund” and “money back”. A rewrite is usually a language model call, and a small one is enough.

9 · TWO SEARCHES

The rewritten query goes two ways at once

Keyword search gives a list ranked by BM25. Meaning search gives a list ranked by cosine. Each list is good at something the other misses: the keyword list is strict about words, and the meaning list is generous about them.

Notice the lists are different lengths. BM25 only returns documents that share a word with the query. The meaning list ranks all five, because every document has a position on the map.

9 · HYBRID SEARCH

Combine the two lists

Hybrid search runs both searches and merges the results. The usual merge is reciprocal rank fusion. Each document gets 1 divided by (60 plus its rank) from every list it appears in, and the scores are added.

A document near the top of both lists rises fastest. The money-back article is first on both, so it ends up first. You never need to make the two scores comparable: only the ranks are used.

9 · RERANKING

Read the best candidates twice

A reranker takes the top candidates and reads each one together with the question, instead of comparing two separate vectors. It is slower, so it runs only on the top few. It is usually more accurate, and it is a common way to lift quality without changing the index.

Here the cut line keeps the top two for the prompt. The reranker’s own scores are not shown, because we did not run one. What matters is the place it has in the pipeline.

9 · CONFIGURATION

The knobs, and what each one trades

k is how many passages reach the prompt. More gives the model more to work with, and more to be distracted by. Chunk size and overlap were covered in the last act. The keyword ↔ meaning weight decides how much each list counts when they are not simply fused. Rerank top N is how many candidates the reranker reads.

Change one knob at a time. A search with four changes at once cannot tell you which one helped.

in

a rewritten query, two rankings, and a few settings

out

the best passages, in order, for the prompt

means

each setting trades quality against speed and cost. Measure each change.

query rewriting · fan-out and drift

DEEP DIVE · ACT NINE

Rewrite when the words are the problem

A rewrite is worth it when the user’s words are not the document’s words, the question depends on earlier turns, or it is very short. Do not rewrite a clear query that already finds its documents. Multi-query fans the question out and merges the results, which lifts recall at the cost of more searches.

multi-query: 3 phrasings → 3 searches → merge with RRF
HyDE:        draft an answer → embed the draft → search with it

DRIFT

Check that a rewrite did not wander

Jaccard = |A ∩ B| / |A ∪ B|

A (original)  = {D4, D1}
B (rewrite)   = {D1, D4, D3}  → 2 / 3 = 0.67   keep it
B (drifted)   = {D3, D2}      → 0 / 5 = 0.00   drop it

Run this on every labelled question, with and without the rewrite. A rewrite that keeps the original’s good documents and adds a few is helping. One that shares nothing has changed the question.

fusion · RRF and weighted scores

DEEP DIVE · ACT TEN

Reciprocal rank fusion

RRF(d) = Σ over lists L containing d:  1 / (60 + rank_L(d))

D4: 1/61 + 1/61 = 0.0328
D1: 1/62 + 1/62 = 0.0323
D3: 1/63        = 0.0159

No scores are compared, only ranks, so the two lists need no common scale. The constant 60 damps the top ranks. The cost: a document far ahead in one list gains only a little more than one slightly ahead.

WEIGHTED

Scale each list, then choose how much each counts

norm(s)   = (s − min) / (max − min)
hybrid(d) = α · norm_keyword(d) + (1 − α) · norm_meaning(d)

Min-max puts both lists on 0..1. α is the weight on keywords. Tune it on labelled questions: exact product codes favour keywords, descriptive questions favour meaning. Re-check α when the corpus changes a lot, because one outlier score squashes everything else.

reranking · cost and the cut-off

DEEP DIVE · ACT ELEVEN

A reranker reads the question with each candidate

A cross-encoder scores one (question, passage) pair per forward pass. It is more accurate than comparing two independent vectors, because it reads both texts together. It is also slower: cost grows with the number of candidates, on every question.

cost per query   ≈ N · cost_per_pair
latency         ≈ N · time_per_pair   (when run one at a time)

THE CUT-OFF

Score is not the decision; the threshold is

Once you have scores, set a threshold on labelled data: answer when the best candidate clears it, otherwise say no answer. Scores here are the cosine values from Act 5, standing in for a reranker’s scores, to show the mechanism. Rerank a candidate set of 20 to 50, keep 3 to 8 for the prompt, and check precision and answer quality with and without the reranker.

Try it · step by step

  1. Scrub the query fanning out into three phrasings, and the Jaccard overlap.
  2. Open Lab 9 · RRF and weighted scores side by side. Keep the default lists: keyword D4, D1; meaning D4, D1, D3, D5, D2.
  3. Drag α from 0 to 1 and watch the weighted order change.

What to expect. RRF gives D4 first, D1 second, then D3, D5, D2. In the weighted column, α is the weight on meaning: at α = 0, D3, D5 and D2 score zero; at α = 1, only the meaning list counts.

Check yourself. Move D3 to the top of the keyword list. Does RRF put D3 first? What does that show about RRF ignoring score size?

measuring retrieval · recall@k and precision@k

ACT TEN

Measuring retrieval

How do you know the search is good? You need two things: a list of questions with the documents that should answer them, and a way to count how many of those documents came back.

Here is a ranked result for one question. Two documents are marked relevant. The ranking is the system’s guess; the marks are the truth we decided in advance.

10 · k

Look only at the first k results

Users rarely read past the first few results, and a RAG prompt only takes the top few passages. So we measure the first k. Move the cut line down the list and the measurements change with it.

10 · PRECISION@k

Of what you showed, how much was right?

Precision@k is the share of the first k results that are relevant. At k = 1, the top result is relevant: 1.00. At k = 2, one of two is relevant: 0.50. At k = 3, two of three are: 0.67.

High precision can hide a problem. If k is too small, precision looks perfect and the answer is still missing.

10 · RECALL@k

Of what was right, how much did you find?

Recall@k is the share of all relevant documents that appear in the first k. There are two relevant documents, so at k = 1 we have found one of two: 0.50. At k = 3 we have found both: 1.00.

The two measures pull against each other. Raising k raises recall and lowers precision. Choosing k is choosing which failure you accept.

in

a ranked list, a set of relevant documents, and a value of k

out

precision@k and recall@k, each between 0 and 1

means

precision asks “was what I showed right?” and recall asks “did I miss anything?”

nDCG and MRR

DEEP DIVE · ACT TWELVE

nDCG: order matters, and answers differ in quality

DCG@k  = Σ_i (2^rel_i − 1) / log2(i + 1)
nDCG@k = DCG@k / IDCG@k

relevance: D4 = 2, D1 = 1
ranking [D1, D5, D4, D2, D3]:
DCG@5  = 1/log2(2) + 3/log2(4) = 1 + 1.5 = 2.5
IDCG@5 = 3/log2(2) + 1/log2(3) = 3 + 0.63 = 3.63
nDCG@5 = 2.5 / 3.63 = 0.69

Gain doubles with each grade, and the discount falls with rank. The bars show each position’s contribution. Scrub to see the ideal order.

MRR

When one good answer is all that matters

RR(q)  = 1 / rank of the first relevant result
MRR    = mean of RR(q) over questions

first relevant at rank 1 and rank 3:
MRR = (1 + 1/3) / 2 = 0.667

MRR is right for a chatbot that quotes one passage. It ignores everything after the first hit, so use nDCG when the whole list is read.

Try it · step by step

  1. Scrub precision falling and recall rising as k changes.
  2. Open Lab 10 · Precision, recall, MRR, nDCG. Keep the relevant documents as D1 and D4. Set k to 1, 3 and 5 and read precision and recall.
  3. Lower D4’s grade to 1 and read nDCG.

What to expect. At k = 1: precision 1.00, recall 0.50. At k = 3: precision 0.67, recall 1.00. Lowering D4’s grade from 2 to 1 raises nDCG to about 0.92, because the two relevant documents are then worth the same.

Check yourself. At which k does recall reach 1.00, and what is precision at that k?

measuring answers · evals and the judge

ACT ELEVEN

Measuring answers

Retrieval has a score. The answer does too, and it is harder to score. Two kinds of evaluation do the work: a search eval checks the passages, and an LLM eval checks the answer.

Both start with a golden set: questions written by people, each with the document or answer that should come back.

11 · LLM-AS-A-JUDGE

A model grades the answers against a rubric

Reading thousands of answers is slow. So a second model, the judge, reads each answer with the passage it was built from, and grades it against a written rubric. The three checks on the stage are a common start: does the answer stay with the passage, does it answer the question, does it cite where it came from.

A judge can be fooled by a confident, fluent answer. Its rubric must ask for evidence, not for tone.

11 · CHECKING THE JUDGE

A judge you have not checked is an opinion

Before you trust a judge, have a person label a sample of answers, a sample you can label in an afternoon, such as twenty to fifty. Then measure how often the judge and the person agree. If they agree most of the time, the judge is useful. If not, fix the rubric and check again.

in

a golden set, the system’s answers, and a rubric

out

a score for each answer, and an agreement rate for the judge

means

retrieval scores tell you whether the right passage arrived; answer scores tell you whether the model used it well

judge agreement · kappa and bias

DEEP DIVE · ACT THIRTEEN

Agreement that means something

p_o = (8 + 6) / 20 = 0.70          observed agreement
p_e = (11/20)(11/20) + (9/20)(9/20) = 0.505   chance agreement
κ   = (p_o − p_e) / (1 − p_e) = 0.39

Seventy percent agreement looks good, but a judge that says “pass” most of the time agrees by chance too. Kappa takes that out. 0.39 is only fair. Fix the rubric where the six disagreements happen, then re-label a fresh sample.

BIAS

Swap the order and see if the verdict moves

Judges favour whichever answer comes first. Show the pair both ways round, count a win only when both orders agree, and report how often the verdict flipped. Verbosity and self-preference are the other two biases to test.

Try it · step by step

  1. Scrub the golden set and the judge’s rubric checks.
  2. Open Lab 11 · Judge agreement with kappa. Keep the counts 8, 3, 3, 6 and read p_o, p_e and κ.
  3. Change the counts and read κ again. Try 75% agreement on 20 items with 60% chance agreement.

What to expect. With 8, 3, 3, 6: p_o = 0.70, p_e = 0.505, κ = 0.39, which is fair. Agreement counts only to the extent it beats chance.

Check yourself. Is 75% agreement with 60% chance agreement good? Compute κ by hand.

the working system

ACT TWELVE

Everything, working together

Two pipelines share one index. The top row prepares the documents, once and ahead of time. The bottom row answers each question, every time someone asks.

12 · ONE QUESTION, TRACED

Follow the money-back question through

The question is rewritten, searched by both methods, fused, and reranked. The best passages come out of the index and go into the prompt. The model answers from them and cites the one it used.

Every step is a place where the answer can go wrong, and every step can be measured on its own. That is why the evals in the last two acts were built around each stage.

12 · COST AND SPEED

Most of the time goes to the model

Searching the index is cheap and quick. Embedding the question and reranking the top few cost a little more. The language model call is the slowest step and usually the most expensive. These figures are illustrative: measure your own system before deciding anything.

The cheapest way to make answers better is often a better retrieval, because it stops the model from paying to read the wrong passages.

12 · FRESHNESS

An index is only as new as its last rebuild

Documents change. An article is edited, but the index still holds the old text until it is rebuilt. Between the edit and the rebuild, the system answers with the old wording. Choose a refresh schedule that matches how often your documents change.

in

documents, a model, a schedule, a question

out

a cited answer, built from the index as it stood

means

cost, speed and freshness trade against each other; every one of them is a setting you choose

latency and cost · what adds up

DEEP DIVE · ACT FOURTEEN

Where the time goes

latency ≈ embed(query)
        + max(keyword time, meaning time)   (in parallel)
        + fusion
        + rerank(N)
        + LLM time to first token
        + output tokens / tokens per second

Reranking grows with every candidate. The LLM is usually the largest single term. Illustrative timings, not measurements: measure your own system.

COST PER QUESTION

Every passage is paid for, every time

cost ≈ embed_price · query_tokens
     + rerank_price · N
     + llm_price_in · prompt_tokens
     + llm_price_out · output_tokens

prompt_tokens = instructions + k · tokens_per_passage + question

The number of passages is a budget decision. Cut k and the bill falls linearly. Pick k from the budget first, then confirm it with your evals.

Try it · step by step

  1. Scrub the whole system and follow one question from the right to the answer.
  2. Open Lab 12 · Where the time and tokens go. Keep 3 passages and 20 candidates. Read tokens and rerank time.
  3. Raise candidates to 50, then passages to 8, and read again.

What to expect. About 1,530 tokens per question with 3 passages. 20 rerank pairs at 20 ms is 400 ms. Both grow linearly; the model call is usually the largest cost at scale, and the lab does not time it.

Check yourself. Which knob would you cut first to halve the cost, and what would you expect to lose?

Glossary

The words, in one place

Token
A piece of a word that a model reads. Roughly three quarters of an English word.
Inverted index
A table from each word to the documents that contain it. It makes keyword lookup fast.
Term frequency
How many times a word appears in one document.
Inverse document frequency
A weight that is higher for words found in few documents.
BM25
The standard keyword ranking. Scores matches by rarity, saturation and length.
Embedding
A list of numbers that places a piece of text by its meaning.
Dimension
One number in an embedding. Real models use 384 to 3,072 of them.
Cosine similarity
The cosine of the angle between two vectors. Near 1 means the same direction; length is ignored.
Vector search
Finding the stored vectors nearest to the question’s vector.
Brute force
Comparing the question with every stored vector. Exact, and slow at very large scale.
HNSW
An index for vectors that finds near neighbours quickly, at a small cost in exactness.
Chunk
A piece of a document that is embedded and indexed on its own.
Overlap
Words repeated at the edge of neighbouring chunks, so no idea is cut in two.
Context window
The most text a model can read in one request.
RAG
Retrieval-augmented generation: find passages first, then answer from them.
Intent
What the user wants, such as a refund request.
Query rewriting
Turning the user’s question into the words the documents use.
Hybrid search
Running keyword and meaning search, then merging the two lists.
Reciprocal rank fusion
A merge that adds 1 ÷ (60 + rank) from each list.
Reranker
A model that reads each question and candidate passage together, to reorder the top few.
Precision@k
Of the first k results, the share that are relevant.
Recall@k
Of all relevant documents, the share found in the first k results.
Golden set
Questions with the documents or answers that should come back, written by people.
LLM-as-a-judge
A model that grades answers against a rubric. Check it against people first.
Latency
How long one request takes, from question to answer.
Freshness
How up to date the index is with the documents it was built from.
