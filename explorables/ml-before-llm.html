A model can be 90% accurate and catch almost nobody

Kestrel Bikes wants to know which subscription customers will cancel next month, so the retention team can call them. A vendor demo reports 90% accuracy. The team buys it. Three months later, churn has not moved at all.

Out of 600 customers, 71 actually cancel. What accuracy would you score by predicting, with no model at all, that nobody ever cancels?

88.2%. That is the number to beat, and it comes from a program with no model in it: one line that says no. This is the base rate problem, and it is the first thing a week of ordinary machine learning teaches you. Accuracy counts how often you are right, which is a nearly useless measure when one answer is right 88% of the time by default.

The trained model in the grid above scores 89.0%. Barely above the do-nothing line. Worse, look at who it catches.

Fig. 2 · Pick a predictor. The four counts are the whole of model evaluation, and every one of them is a different business consequence.

At its default setting the model finds 15 of the 71 leavers. Recall, the share of real leavers it catches, is 21%. The retention team was handed a list that misses four out of five people they were trying to save, and the accuracy score on the slide never mentioned it.

A smoke alarm that never goes off is right on almost every day of your life. Its accuracy over a year is close to perfect and it is worth nothing, because the only day it is graded on is the day there is a fire. When the thing you care about is rare, being right on average and being useful are different achievements.

None of this is about language models, and that is the point. Anyone who skips straight to prompting a chatbot for predictions inherits every one of these traps, plus a system that cannot even show you its four counts.

So the model is poor. Except it is not: the same model, with one number changed and no retraining at all, finds more than half the leavers. That number is not part of the model.

The threshold is a business decision wearing a technical costume

The model does not output "will leave". It outputs a probability for each customer. Turning that into a phone-call list needs a cut-off, and choosing the cut-off is choosing how many people you are willing to call for nothing in order to save one who would have left.

Put the retention offer's cost and a saved customer's value in, then drag the cut-off until the money is best.

Fig. 3 · Same trained model throughout. Only the cut-off moves. Purple tiles are customers the model flags; red tiles are leavers it missed.

At 0.5 the model flags 25 people and saves the ones it catches. Drop the cut-off to 0.2 and it flags 96, catching 38 of the 71 leavers, and at a 15 pound offer against a 200 pound customer, that trade is strongly worth making. Accuracy went down. Profit went up. Those two sentences are the reason accuracy is the wrong slide.

Precision is the other half of the trade: of the people you call, what share would really have left. Precision and recall move against each other, always, and the honest way to present a model is both numbers at the cut-off you actually intend to use.

Airport security screening is tuned, not solved. Loosen it and you stop more threats while searching more grandmothers; tighten it and the queue moves while more gets through. Nobody thinks there is a "correct" setting hidden in the scanner, because everyone can see the two costs. A churn model has exactly the same dial, and it is usually left at whatever the library's default was.

A probability you can set a cut-off on is worth more than a label. That is only true if the probability means something, and there is a specific test for whether it does.

A probability is only useful if 30% means it happens three times in ten

Group every customer the model scored between 0.2 and 0.4, then look at how many of them actually left. If the model is honest, the answer should be around 30%. This test is called calibration, and it takes one chart.

Fig. 4 · Tap a bucket to see the customers in it. The diagonal is perfect calibration: predicted on one axis, what actually happened on the other.

A calibrated score lets you do arithmetic with it: expected loss, expected revenue, how many offers to budget for. An uncalibrated one cannot be multiplied by anything, so every downstream decision is guesswork with decimal places.

Now bring that habit to a language model. Ask one for a percentage and it will produce a confident-looking number, but that number is text it generated, not a measured frequency, and it has not been checked against any outcome. Nothing stops you from checking it, and hardly anyone does. Someone who has drawn this chart once asks for it forever after, which is exactly the instinct that makes an LLM project survive contact with reality.

A weather forecaster who says 30% rain is graded over a hundred such days: it should rain on about thirty of them. That grading is why forecasts are trustworthy in a way that a friend's "probably fine" is not. The number is not the claim; the track record of the number is the claim.

Say you have fixed all of that: sensible baseline, tuned cut-off, calibrated scores. There is one failure left that makes a model look better than any of this, and it is the one that fools whole teams.

The fastest way to a perfect model is to cheat by accident

An analyst adds a promising column to the churn data: days_since_cancel_form_opened. Accuracy jumps to 99.8%. The team celebrates. In production the model flags nobody, because at the moment of prediction that field is always empty. It is only filled in by people who are already leaving.

Switch the column on and off. The left bar is the score on held-back data, the right is the same model on next month's live data, where the future has not happened yet.

Fig. 5 · Toggle the columns the model is allowed to use. Everything here is retrained in your browser when you click.

That is leakage: information in your training data that would not be available at the moment you need the prediction. It has sunk more projects than any modelling mistake, and no amount of model sophistication detects it, because as far as the maths is concerned the column is simply excellent.

The defence is a habit, not a technique. Hold back data the model never sees during training (the train-test split), split it by time when the task is about the future, and for every column ask one question: would this value exist, in this state, at the moment I have to decide.

A student who studies from a textbook with the answers pencilled in the margin scores brilliantly on the practice paper. The pencil marks are not knowledge, and there is no way to tell from the practice score alone. The only test that means anything is the one written by someone who could not see your notes.

The same trap reappears in the LLM world wearing new clothes: a model evaluated on benchmark questions that were in its training data, or a RAG system tested with the exact passages its retriever was tuned on. The word is contamination there, the mechanism is identical, and the person who has been burned by a leaking column once recognises it immediately.

All four of these habits came from problems that have nothing to do with text. Which raises the fair question: how much of your actual work is text?

Most of the value in a company sits in tables, not paragraphs

Pick any five decisions your business repeats daily. Which customer to call, which invoice to chase, what to order, what price to set, which transaction to hold. Each one arrives as rows and columns, with a right answer recorded later. That shape is what classical machine learning was built for, and on it a gradient-boosted tree still beats a language model on accuracy, cost and speed, usually by a lot.

Now the running cost, which decides what you are allowed to build. Score a million rows a night with a tree model and it costs pennies of compute and finishes over coffee. Send the same million rows through a frontier language model and you are paying per token and waiting on network calls.

A courier bike and a cargo plane both move parcels. Nobody argues about which is more advanced, because the question is what you are shipping and how far. Sending one envelope across town by cargo plane is not a bold technology choice, it is a bill.

The nuance, honestly: language models are getting cheaper fast, they are unbeatable when the input is messy free text, and a small distilled model can sit in the middle. The judgement of which one fits is exactly what classical training gives you, because it is the only training that makes you compare against a cheap baseline before reaching for the expensive thing.

Fine, you might say, but my problem really is text, so none of this applies to me. It applies more, not less, and here is the part that surprises people.

Every idea inside a language model is one of these ideas, scaled up

An LLM is not a separate technology that happens to share a research field. It is the same machinery: a loss, gradient descent, layers of weights, a train and test split, overfitting, embeddings. Learning them on a 600-row spreadsheet takes a weekend. Meeting them for the first time inside a 500 billion parameter model takes months, because you cannot see anything.

The transfer runs the other way too. Every serious language model project ends up needing the classical parts: an evaluation set with labels, a threshold on a classifier that decides whether to escalate, a retrieval step that is plain vector search, a cost model, and a measurement of whether last week's change helped. Teams without that grounding tend to ship prompts and hope, and they are the ones whose pilots produce a good demo and no number.

A pianist learning jazz does not skip scales because improvisation sounds more exciting. The scales are not a prerequisite ritual, they are the material the improvisation is made from. When the solo goes somewhere unexpected, the scales are what let you follow.

The prompt is the easy part. Knowing whether the output is any good is the job, and that skill is entirely classical.

All of which sounds like an argument for two years of study before you are allowed to touch an API. It is not, and that version of the advice does real harm.

Depth is a prerequisite for judgement, not for starting

The honest counter-argument, and it is a good one: people learn far better with a working thing in their hands. Nobody learns to cook by studying food chemistry. fast.ai built a whole teaching philosophy on this, starting students with a working image classifier in lesson one and the theory afterwards, and it produces capable practitioners faster than the bottom-up route.

So the claim on this page is narrower than "study three years first". It is about which parts you cannot skip, and when. Drag the slider to see what each level of grounding does and does not let you do.

Fig. 9 · Four levels of grounding. The lists are what you can honestly claim and what will quietly bite you.

Three things you genuinely do not need before building with an LLM: the maths of backpropagation, the ability to implement a transformer, and a course in statistics. Three things you do need before anyone relies on your output: a baseline to compare against, a labelled set of cases to measure on, and the habit of asking what would have to be true for this number to be lying to you. The first list takes years and is optional. The second list takes about two weeks and is not.

A driving licence is not an engineering degree. You are not asked to explain combustion, but you are asked to know what a wet road does to stopping distance, because that is the knowledge that shows up in the moment something goes wrong. Skip the engine, keep the stopping distances.

There is also a career point worth saying plainly. Prompting is the fastest-commoditising skill in the industry; the models absorb prompt craft with every release. Knowing whether a system works, and being able to prove it with an evaluation set and a baseline, has not commoditised at all, and it is the same skill it was in 2015.

Which leaves the practical question: in what order, and how long does each part actually take?

Two weeks of fundamentals, then build the thing you wanted to build

Tell the builder below who you are and what you want to make. It produces an order, with the reason each step is where it is. Nothing in it takes longer than a fortnight before you are building again.

Fig. 10 · Pick a role and a goal. The path changes: some people genuinely should start at the API and come back for the rest.

Whatever path you get, one exercise is worth more than any course on it. Take a decision your team makes weekly, write down 30 past cases with what actually happened, and try to beat the obvious rule. You will hit the base rate, the threshold, the leaking column and the calibration question in a single afternoon, on data you care about, and after that none of them are abstract again.

Nobody remembers the chapter on humidity. Everyone remembers the loaf that did not rise, and why. One real failure on your own data installs more judgement than ten tutorials on someone else's.

Then go and build the language model thing. It will be better, because you will be able to tell when it is not.

The fundamentals are not a gate you pass to earn the fun part. They are the only reason you will know whether the fun part worked.

Into the weeds

Every figure on this page runs on one synthetic customer base, generated from a fixed seed so your numbers match the ones in the text. 500 customers for training, 600 held back for testing, of whom 71 (11.8%) cancel.

Truth used to generate the data (the model never sees it): z = -2.9 - 0.045·tenure + 0.55·tickets + 0.75·late_payments - 0.010·spend + 0.6·premium_plan churn ~ Bernoulli( sigmoid(z) ) The model fitted in your browser: logistic regression, 5 features scaled to roughly 0..1 loss L = -(1/n) Σ [ y·log p + (1-y)·log(1-p) ], p = sigmoid(w·x + b) fitted by batch gradient descent, learning rate 0.6, 4000 steps Measures: accuracy = (TP+TN)/n recall = TP/(TP+FN) precision = TP/(TP+FP) expected value = TP·(value - cost) - FP·cost calibration: bucket by predicted p, compare bucket mean of p with observed rate The leaking column: days_since_cancel_form_opened is non-zero only for customers who churn, which is knowable only after the decision point. Offline it looks perfect.

Why logistic regression rather than a tree: it is the model whose parameters you can read, its probabilities are close to calibrated without extra work, and it fits in twenty lines of JavaScript, so nothing on this page is hidden behind a library.

Sources and honesty notes. The customer base is synthetic, and it is generated rather than borrowed so that the leakage and base-rate effects are exactly reproducible. All figures compute their numbers live from that data in your browser. The studies referenced in the companion field guide are measured; nothing on this page claims to measure your business.
