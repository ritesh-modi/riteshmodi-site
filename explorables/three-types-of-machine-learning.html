A chef, a recipe box,and a restaurant.

Three ways to learn to cook — and the three kinds of machine learning, in the same order. What separates them is not how clever they are or when they were invented. It is one question: when you try something, what comes back?

Three ways to learn to cook

Do not start with definitions. Start here, because it carries all three and you will keep coming back to it.

Cooking classes

A chef stands next to you. You cook, the chef tastes it and says “too much salt” or “perfect”. Every single attempt comes back with the right answer attached. After a hundred dishes you have internalised the chef’s judgement.

What made that possible: somebody who already knew the answer, willing to tell you, every time.

The unlabelled recipe box

Someone hands you five hundred recipes with the titles torn off and says: “tell me something about these.”

There is no right answer. Nobody is going to mark your work. But you would still notice things — a lot of these use tomato, garlic and basil; that pile is all baked; those over there take under fifteen minutes. You have found structure that nobody put there for you.

What made that possible: nothing but the data itself. No chef, no answers, no marking.

Opening a restaurant

Nobody tells you what to cook. You put dishes on the menu and find out, weeks later, from whether people come back and what they order again.

You never get told “the biryani should have had more saffron”. You only get the till receipts. So you experiment — change one thing, see what happens, keep what works, drop what does not. Slowly you get good, and you still could not fully explain why.

What made that possible: the freedom to try things, and consequences you could measure.

Chef is supervised. Recipe box is unsupervised. Restaurant is reinforcement.

These are not three different technologies. They are three different situations you can be in, defined entirely by what kind of feedback is available to you. The situation picks the approach — you don’t.
worth trying on yourselfIf you wanted to teach someone your job, which of the three would it be? Most people say “I’d sit with them and correct their work” — supervised. Then ask: what about the parts of your job where you cannot say why you do it that way, only whether it worked? That is the other two.

When somebody already knows the answer

You have a pile of examples where the right answer is already known, and the machine learns to reproduce it on cases it has not seen. The most important word here is labelled. A label is the right answer, sitting next to the example. Forty cars and what each sold for. Ten customers and whether each one cancelled.

Where those labels come from is a business problem, not a technical one:

SourceExampleCost
Reality provides them, freeThe customer cancelled or didn’t. The parcel arrived at 4:12pm. The loan was repaid.Free — but you must have recorded it, and most organisations didn’t

Having data is not the requirement. Having data with the answers attached is the requirement. Twenty years of invoices with no record of which customers turned out to be trouble gives you nothing to learn from.

Does the answer come out as a word, or as a number?

That single question splits supervised learning in two, and it is far more useful than the textbook vocabulary. Here is the test that settles it every time: could you write all the possible answers on a menu? If yes, the answer is a word from a list. If no — if they slide along a scale — the answer is a number.

Ask it another way: is there anything in between two answers? Between ten lakh and eleven lakh there is 10.5, and 10.51, and 10.513, infinitely many. So that is a number. Between “spam” and “not spam” there is nothing at all — you cannot be 40% of the way from spam to not-spam. So that is a word.

The part almost nobody teaches

Take one customer and one set of data. Watch what happens when you change nothing except how you phrase the question.

same customer, same data — you choose the question

Same customer. Same data. Same information available. Different types of machine learning — because you chose how to ask.

Two things follow. The type is chosen by the question, not by the data. Anyone who tells you “our data is a classification problem” has skipped a decision that was theirs to make.

And turning a number into a band throws information away — sometimes on purpose. A precise figure and a band are not equally useful. The band is easier to act on, easier to explain, and easier to get right. Simplifying the question is a legitimate and often smart move.

the question behind the questionWhich version would you rather hand a sales team on a Monday morning? Most people say the band. Now which would you rather have for the annual budget? Most switch to the precise number. The right type depends on what the answer is for.

One piece of vocabulary worth being blunt about: the formal name for predicting a number is regression, which is a historical accident from 19th-century statistics and has nothing to do with going backwards. It just means predicting a number. Saying so plainly buys more credibility than pretending the name makes sense.

When nobody knows the answer

You have data and no answers at all, and you are asking the machine to find structure that nobody labelled. There is no cancelled column. No price column. No answer of any kind, anywhere.

That is genuinely a different activity. With a chef, reality told us the answer and we learned to match it. Here nothing does — so there is nothing to be “right” about.

Supervised learning answers a question you asked. Unsupervised learning tells you something you did not know to ask.

What groups are in here?

Think of sorting laundry. Nobody gives you the categories. You just start putting like with like, and piles emerge — whites, colours, delicates. Different people would make slightly different piles, and none of them is wrong.

That last point matters and is worth stating plainly: clustering has no right answer. Ask for two groups and you get two. Ask for five and you get five. The machine will always oblige. Whether the groups are useful is a human judgement, and it is yours.

Where it earns its keep: customer segments nobody defined in a marketing meeting; which branches actually behave alike, regardless of region; products bought by the same kind of person; themes across thousands of complaints.

The punchline is worth sitting with. A group like “visits twice a year, spends a fortune” is completely invisible on a visits-per-month report. Nobody would have defined that segment in a meeting. The data had it all along — clustering just made somebody look.

What does not belong?

A different job: not sorting into groups, but looking for the single thing that should not be there. The red sock in the white wash. A transaction that does not look like you. A machine vibrating slightly wrong before it fails. A login from a country you have never been to.

Here is the puzzle worth pausing on. Fraud is just yes or no — so why not take past frauds, label them, and learn what fraud looks like? Two reasons, and both are deep.

You do not have examples of what you are looking for. The fraud that will hurt you next year has not happened yet. A classifier can only recognise patterns it has already been shown, so it is permanently one step behind.

Accuracy is a broken measure when one answer is overwhelmingly common. So anomaly detection flips the question entirely:

Classification learns what the thing looks like. Anomaly detection learns what everything else looks like, and points at whatever is left over.

You need no examples of fraud at all. You only need lots of examples of normal — which you have, by the million, for free.

The cost of that flip, and it is worth being honest about: anomaly detection finds things that are unusual, and unusual is not the same as wrong. Your first-ever trip abroad is genuinely anomalous and entirely legitimate. That is why these systems generate so many false alarms, and why a human sits at the end of almost all of them.

When you only find out afterwards

Nobody tells you the right answer; you try things, and you find out later how it went. Reward and punishment is the whole vocabulary you need.

Training a dog is the clearest version. You never explain anything. The dog sits and gets a treat. Jumps on the sofa and gets none. Nobody has ever described the rule, and the dog could not state it — but within weeks the behaviour is exactly what you wanted.

Or learning to ride a bicycle: no one can give you instructions precise enough to follow. You wobble, you fall, you adjust. The feedback is falling over.

Two things that make it hard

Which move was the good one? You win a chess game on move sixty. Which of your sixty moves won it? The reward arrives at the end, attached to everything you did. This is the single hardest thing in the field.

Every executive recognises the business version: a large deal closes after eight meetings, three emails and a dinner. Which one closed it? Nobody knows. That is not a failure of record-keeping — the credit genuinely cannot be assigned from a single outcome.

The known-good, or the untried? Friday night. Your favourite restaurant, which you know is good — or the new place, which might be better and might be terrible?

Go to the favourite every time and you will never find anything better. Try somewhere new every time and you will eat a lot of bad dinners. Every reinforcement learning system faces this at every single step, and so does every business:

Stick with what worksTry something new
Keep running the ad that worksTest a new one
Sell to the segment we understandTry the segment we don’t
Keep the pricing that’s fineExperiment and risk a bad quarter
a board-level question long before it is a technical oneWhat share of your budget goes to trying things that might not work? For most organisations the honest answer is “almost none, and we call that discipline.”

Why you rarely see it in business

Be straight about this or the idea oversells itself. Reinforcement learning needs somewhere safe to fail thousands of times. A simulated robot can fall over a million times before lunch. A pricing algorithm cannot destroy a million real transactions to find out what works.

Works brilliantlyStruggles
Games, where the rules are known and failure is freeAnything where each mistake costs real money
Robotics and logistics — you can simulate firstAnything a regulator must approve
Ad and offer selection — millions of cheap, fast, reversible decisionsAnything where feedback takes months

The pattern: it thrives where mistakes are cheap, fast and reversible. Most business decisions are none of those three.

Where you have already met it

A raw language model, trained on a mountain of text, is good at predicting the next word and not naturally good at being helpful, safe or willing to follow an instruction. What fixed that was reinforcement learning: the model produces several answers to the same question, humans rank them — this one is more helpful, that one is rude — the model is rewarded for producing the preferred kind of answer, and that repeats, enormously.

Predicting the next word made it knowledgeable. Reinforcement made it useful.

Two things worth adding. The ranking is labour: thousands of people reading answers and choosing between them. That is the hidden human cost inside every polished AI product. And you get what you reward — a model rewarded for answers that look good learns to produce answers that look good, which is one honest explanation of why these systems sometimes sound confident about things they have invented.

Which one is your problem?

Work down and stop at the first yes. Do you already know the right answer for past cases, and did somebody write it down? If yes, you are supervised — and then, is the answer one of a short list you could put on a menu, or a number that could land anywhere on a scale?

If no: are you looking for the odd one out rather than sorting everything into groups? Or do you just want to know what natural groups exist?

And if there is no answer key at all — only consequences that arrive later, and you can afford to experiment — that is reinforcement. If you cannot afford to experiment either, you may not have a machine learning problem yet.

That very first question is the one that stops most real projects. Not “do we have data” but do we have the answers, written down, for past cases? Organisations routinely discover at exactly this point that nobody ever recorded what happened next.

The two loan questions are the whole exercise: identical data, identical customer, and the type changed because the question changed. If you keep one thing from this page, keep that.

And the star rating is genuinely ambiguous. Treat one-to-five stars as five labels and you cannot express 4.5. Treat it as a number and the model may output 3.7 stars, which nobody ever gave. Both choices are defensible and both lose something. Real problems sit on this boundary constantly.

What this does not settle

Two limits worth carrying away.

The neat five-box table hides how often real problems straddle boxes. The star rating above is not a trick — it is typical. Plenty of live systems chain several types together: cluster first to find segments, then run a separate classifier inside each one. The table is a way to think, not a filing system.

And picking the right type does not make the problem solvable. You can identify correctly that you have a supervised classification problem and still fail, because nobody recorded the labels, or the labels that exist encode a decision you would not want repeated. Which kind of problem you have and whether you can act on it are separate questions, and this page only answers the first.

Three sentences to keep. These are three situations, not three technologies — the feedback available to you picks the approach. The type is chosen by the question you ask, not by the data you hold. And having data is not the requirement; having data with the answers attached is the requirement.
