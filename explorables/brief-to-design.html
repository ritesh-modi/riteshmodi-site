The translation problem

Clients speak Business. Research speaks Questions. They are different languages.

A business objective is a statement about what an organisation wants to achieve. A research question is a statement about what you need to understand to help them get there. These are not the same sentence with different words — they operate on entirely different levels.

Business objectives describe outcomes in the world: revenue, market share, product adoption, customer retention. Research questions describe gaps in human understanding: what people believe, how they make decisions, what needs go unmet, what experiences shape behaviour. The translation is the work.

Picture it A doctor doesn't ask "how do I make this patient healthier?" — that's the outcome. They ask "what is causing the symptoms?" — that's the diagnostic question. The research question is always the diagnostic question. The business objective is always the health outcome. Your job is to write the diagnostic question well enough that answering it genuinely moves toward the outcome.

The failure mode is skipping this step — taking the client's language and treating it as if it were already a research question. When that happens, you design a study that feels relevant but produces findings that cannot be acted upon. The client nods at the findings, files the report, and nothing changes.

Each layer requires a deliberate act of translation. Skipping a layer doesn't make the work faster — it makes the study unable to answer the business question, which means the whole investment is wasted. Let's walk through each translation in turn.

The intake conversation

Before you design anything, you run a five-question diagnostic on the brief.

Most briefs land with a single paragraph and a timeline. Before you can translate it, you need to fill five gaps — gaps the client usually hasn't thought about, and won't think about unless you ask. These five questions are the brief's skeleton. Everything useful in the brief maps to one of them.

The five diagnostic questions

1 · What decision does this enable?

Every research project should make a specific business decision easier. If you can't name the decision, you can't design the study. Ask: "When this research is done, what will you be deciding that you can't decide today?" The answer to this question defines the study's scope ceiling — you only need to learn as much as the decision requires.

2 · What do you already know?

Research begins at the edge of existing knowledge. If the client already has data on usage patterns, brand tracking, sales by segment, or prior qual findings — you don't re-learn it. You start where it ends. Asking this question also reveals whether the client has a hypothesis they haven't admitted to yet, which is extremely useful to know.

3 · Who is the audience we are trying to understand?

Not "who is the target customer" — that's marketing. This question is more specific: whose behaviour, motivations, or perceptions are the research intended to illuminate? Often the research audience and the marketing audience are not the same. A study about why heavy users churn is a study about heavy users who churned — a very specific, often hard-to-reach group.

4 · What does success look like?

Ask the client to describe the ideal research outcome. If they say "we want to know what messaging will resonate" — that's an evaluative question (testing options). If they say "we want to understand what's driving their decision" — that's an exploratory question. The shape of the answer tells you the shape of the study before you've written a single question.

5 · What would change your mind?

This is the most powerful question in any intake conversation. It forces the client to surface their existing assumptions. If they say "nothing — we're pretty sure it's a price issue," you've just discovered the hypothesis. Your research either confirms or challenges it. Either way, that assumed hypothesis is the thing you need to test, not paper over.

Use this in the room Run through these five questions in your first client call — not as a formal questionnaire, but woven into natural conversation. By the time you hang up, you should be able to answer all five. If you can't, schedule a follow-up before you start designing. A study built on incomplete intake is a study that will need to be redesigned mid-field.

Once you have answers to these five questions, you have everything you need to write the first draft of the research question framework — the bridge between the client's world and your study's design.

The core skill

A research question is not a survey question. Writing one well is harder than it looks.

This is the most misunderstood part of the whole process. A research question is a high-level framing of what the study needs to understand. It is not a question you ask participants. It is a question that the study — as a whole — is designed to answer.

Most first-draft research questions fail in one of four ways: they are too broad to be answerable, too narrow to be useful, secretly leading toward a conclusion the client already holds, or abstract to the point where no real human behaviour connects to them.

The three-part test A well-formed research question passes three tests simultaneously: (1) Answerable — a qualitative study can actually shed light on it, (2) Specific enough — the answer will meaningfully narrow the decision space for the client, (3) Open — the answer is genuinely unknown and could go in multiple directions. If a question fails any of these, rewrite it before you design anything.

Building the framework

A single study needs a hierarchy of questions — not one question, not twelve.

The output of the translation process is not one research question — it is a question framework: a primary question that captures the study's central purpose, and three to five sub-questions that subdivide it into researchable parts.

Think of it as a tree. The primary question is the trunk — the study lives or dies by whether it answers this. The sub-questions are branches — they each need airtime in the discussion guide, but none of them is the whole point. The leaves — the specific discussion guide questions and projective exercises — grow from the branches.

Once the framework exists, every other design decision — who to recruit, what method to use, how to structure the sessions — becomes derivable from it. The framework is the load-bearing document in a well-designed study.

From question to method

The research question tells you which method to use. Not the other way around.

The client in our opening brief said "we're thinking focus groups." That's almost certainly the wrong method for a question about why behaviour has changed — but it's an understandable default. Focus groups are the most famous qual method, so they're what people reach for first.

Method selection should flow from the research question, not from client familiarity. Different question types map to different methods because different methods access different kinds of human truth.

On mixed methods: Most real studies use more than one method. A common structure is: exploratory IDIs first (to understand the landscape and generate hypotheses), then focus groups (to pressure-test those hypotheses in a social context), then an online community (to track behaviour or collect stimulus responses over time). The question framework tells you which of these you need — not habit or budget alone.

For the opening brief, the question is fundamentally exploratory — "why is this happening?" — which argues strongly for IDIs over focus groups. In a group, participants anchor on social consensus. In a one-on-one IDI, they give you their actual reasoning, including the uncomfortable parts.

The bottom line

The quality of the research question is the quality of the research.

Everything else — sample design, discussion guide, method, moderation — is execution. Execution matters. But a brilliantly executed study built on the wrong question produces wrong answers faster. Write the question first. Write it right.

Into the weeds

The difference between exploratory, evaluative, and generative questions

Exploratory questions ask "what is going on?" — they are used when the territory is unknown. "What are the unarticulated needs of first-time homebuyers?" You don't know what you don't know. IDIs and ethnographies tend to serve these best because group dynamics in focus groups can suppress genuine outlier perspectives.

Evaluative questions ask "which of these is better, and why?" — they require stimulus material and comparison. "Which of these three value propositions resonates most with our target audience, and what are the reasons?" Focus groups and dyads are excellent here because the comparison dynamic is generative, not suppressive.

Generative questions ask "what should we create?" — they are most common in early-stage innovation. "What does a better banking experience look like for small business owners?" Co-creation sessions, design sprints, and collaborative workshops serve these best. The research and the design are happening simultaneously.

How to handle a client who already has the answer

Confirmation bias in research design is extremely common. A client says "we want to understand our audience" but what they mean is "we want evidence that our strategy is correct." You can tell because they resist any research question that could produce a contrary finding.

The most effective response is to name the hypothesis explicitly in the design document: "The study will test the hypothesis that [client assumption]. If this hypothesis is wrong, the study will reveal it." Clients who are resistant to this framing usually reveal that they don't actually want research — they want validation. That's a different engagement, with different ethics attached to it.

The best researchers develop language for this conversation: "The value of research is that it can tell you things you don't expect. If we design this to only confirm what you already believe, we're not doing research — we're writing a document that looks like research. That's a risk to you, not just to us."

When the research question changes mid-field

It happens. Session three reveals that participants are confused about a basic category concept you assumed they understood. Or a surprising pattern emerges that reframes the whole study. The question is what to do about it.

First: don't ignore it. A finding that contradicts your research question framework is important data. It usually means either the framework was wrong, or the stimulus material revealed something you didn't anticipate.

Second: the discussion guide can be adjusted between sessions as long as the core research question remains intact. Adding a probe, reordering sections, removing a low-yield exercise — all fine. Changing the central question mid-field is rarer and requires a client conversation.

Third: document the pivot. In the final report, note what the original framework assumed and when the data redirected it. This is not weakness — it's intellectual honesty, and clients with research maturity value it.

The proposal section that almost everyone writes wrong

Most qual proposals contain a "Research Objectives" section that is a restatement of the client brief in slightly different language. This is not research objectives — it's paraphrasing. A real research objectives section states the specific questions the study is designed to answer, written in the register of research (not business), and organized hierarchically (primary, then secondary).

Clients often skim this section. They shouldn't. It is the contract — the thing that defines what you will and won't deliver. A well-written research objectives section prevents scope creep ("but we also want to know about X") because X either is or isn't in the section. If it isn't, it wasn't scoped. If it needs to be added, the study needs to be redesigned.

Research questions vs. discussion guide questions — the most common confusion

A research question is abstract and unanswerable by a direct question in a session. "What motivates brand switching in the premium coffee category?" is a research question. You cannot ask a participant that question directly — they'll give you a rationalized, top-of-mind response that misses the motivational reality beneath it.

Discussion guide questions are indirect — they create the conditions under which participants reveal the answer to the research question without being asked it. "Walk me through the last time you tried a coffee brand you'd never bought before. What was going on that day?" — that's a discussion guide question in service of the research question above.

The failure mode is treating them as interchangeable — writing a discussion guide that just lists the research questions as conversation topics. The result is a session that feels like an interview and produces shallow, rationalised responses. The research question framework guides the guide; it does not become the guide.

Stakeholder alignment: who needs to sign off on the framework and when

The research question framework should be reviewed and approved by the client before fieldwork begins — not just the proposal. Practically this means building a brief alignment step into your timeline: after intake, write a one-page "research design brief" that states the question framework clearly, and get explicit sign-off before you write the guide or recruit anyone.

This step is often skipped to save time, and it is almost always the cause of post-field disputes ("this isn't what we wanted to learn"). The sign-off conversation also surfaces stakeholder disagreements on the client side — sometimes the marketing team and the strategy team want different things from the same study, and you'd rather know that before fieldwork than after.
