Qualitative Analysis · The craft of sense-making

Survey analysis:
from transcript to theme

A transcript is not a finding. It is evidence waiting to be interpreted. The journey from a 90-minute recording to a three-slide insight takes more craft than any other part of qual research — and it is almost never taught explicitly.

The core misunderstanding

Analysis is not reading. It is a deliberate act of reduction and abstraction.

When researchers say "we analysed the transcripts," they usually mean one of three things — and two of them aren't really analysis. Reading is the first pass: becoming familiar with what was said. Summarising is tidying up what was said into shorter form. Analysis is the act of moving beyond what was said to identify patterns, tensions, and implications that no single participant stated explicitly.

The insight is never in the transcript. It lives in the space between multiple transcripts — the pattern that emerges only when you can see them all at once.

Picture it Imagine six people each describe their commute to work. Each description is different. Analysis asks: what is structurally similar across all six, despite the surface differences? What tension runs through all of them that none of them named? That structural pattern is the insight — and it doesn't appear in any single transcript.

This explainer walks through the full journey from raw transcript to polished insight — not as an abstract process, but with a real (condensed) dataset you can interact with at each step.

Stage one

Immersion: reading with your researcher brain switched on.

The first read of a transcript is not a passive experience. A trained researcher reads with two questions running simultaneously: what is this person literally saying? and what does this suggest about their underlying experience? Those two questions produce different notes.

Literal content is what the participant said. Latent meaning is what they revealed without saying it. The best qual findings usually come from the latent level — and you can only access it if you notice it during the first read.

Reading like a researcher

Before you read: set your question

Don't open a transcript without a research question in your head. The question is your filter. Without it, everything feels equally interesting — and nothing gets prioritised. Write the primary research question at the top of your analysis document before you open the first transcript.

First pass: marginal reactions

Read without stopping. In the margin (or equivalent), mark moments that feel significant — surprising, unexpected, emotionally charged, or directly relevant to the research question. Don't code yet. Just react. These marks are your first instincts and they're valuable.

Notice what participants DON'T say

Silences are data. Topics that get avoided, questions that get deflected, subjects that everyone talks around — these gaps are often more revealing than what's on the page. Note them explicitly: "P3 never mentioned price, even when asked about trade-offs."

Track emotional register

How does the participant talk about different topics? Flat and matter-of-fact suggests low engagement or resignation. Animated and digressive suggests genuine investment. The shift in register mid-transcript is often where the real insight lives — the moment they move from the performed answer to the real one.

Write a one-paragraph memo after each transcript

Before moving to the next transcript, write 3–5 sentences about what stood out. What was unexpected? What would you most want the client to hear from this person? This memo is not a summary — it's your analytical reaction. It forces synthesis and prevents later transcripts from overwriting your first impressions.

Try it now Before you click into the live transcript in the next section, read this one line and notice your reaction: "I use it every day, but I've never recommended it to anyone." What does that reveal? Write it down before you continue — your first instinct is often your best analytical move.

Once you've read all transcripts and written your memos, you have the raw material for analysis. The next stage is open coding — the first formal pass through the data.

Stage two

Open coding: tagging meaning, not content.

Coding is the practice of attaching a short label — a code — to a segment of text. The code captures what the segment means analytically, not what it describes literally. A participant says "I just didn't bother renewing." The literal description is "non-renewal." The analytical code is something like passive-exit or low-urgency-churn — it names the mechanism, not the event.

Good codes are verbs or verb phrases when possible: they describe what the participant is doing or experiencing, not what they're talking about. "Price sensitivity" is a topic. "Mentally anchoring on a competitor's price" is a code — it describes a cognitive act.

The difference between descriptive and interpretive codes

Descriptive codes label what is present: "product usage," "price mention," "competitor reference." Interpretive codes label what it means: "feature-blindness," "price-as-proxy-for-trust," "competitive framing as self-justification." The difference between a mediocre analysis and a great one is often just this: how far the researcher pushed beyond the descriptive into the interpretive.

"The best code is the one that will still make sense six months from now when you've forgotten the specific participant."

Open coding produces a long, messy list of codes. That's exactly right. The mess is evidence that you're not forcing the data into a predetermined structure — you're letting it speak first. The next stage imposes order.

Stage three

Clustering: finding the groups that the codes are already trying to form.

After open coding, you have a list of 30–80 codes across your full dataset. The next step is to look for codes that seem to belong together — that are describing different facets of the same underlying phenomenon. This process is called axial coding, or more simply, clustering.

The key discipline: you are not imposing clusters. You are discovering them. The question is always "why do these two codes keep appearing near each other?" — not "where should I put this code to make the structure look clean?"

How to tell a real theme from a convenient bucket A real theme has internal coherence — every code in it is describing the same underlying human experience or mechanism. A convenient bucket is just a group of things you didn't know where else to put. Test each theme: can you write a single sentence that captures what all the codes share? If you can't, it's a bucket, not a theme. Break it up and redistribute.

Naming themes: the most underrated skill in qual analysis

A theme name is not a topic label. "Price" is a topic. "Treating price as a proxy for trust" is a theme — it names a relationship, a mechanism, a human behaviour. A good theme name should be surprising enough to make the client think and specific enough to be actionable. If a theme name could appear in any study about any topic, it's not specific enough.

Stage four

Saturation: knowing when you have enough.

Saturation is the point at which new data stops producing new codes or new themes — you're hearing the same things again. It's the signal that your analysis has covered the territory. Before saturation, findings are provisional. After it, you can present them with confidence.

In practice, most qual studies reach thematic saturation between sessions 8 and 14 for a relatively homogeneous audience. For heterogeneous samples or discovery studies, it takes longer. Tracking saturation explicitly — not just feeling it — is how you defend your sample size recommendation to a client who wants to cut it.

Saturation vs. coverage: These are different things. You can have saturation in one theme (everyone says the same thing about price) while another theme is still open (new things keep emerging about brand relationship). True saturation means all major themes have stabilised — not just the most prominent one. Track each theme separately.

Once themes are stable and saturated, you have the analytical foundation. The final step — converting themes into insights — is where the work earns its strategic value.

Stage five — the hardest part

The insight is the theme plus its implication. Without the implication, it's a finding.

A finding reports what happened. An insight explains why it matters and what it means for the client's decision. The difference between them is the "so what" — the sentence that connects the research result to the business context.

Most qual reports are full of findings. The client reads them, nods, and files the document. Insights produce a different reaction: a pause, a moment of recognition, occasionally a room of people saying "I didn't know that." That reaction is what you're building toward.

The so-what test Read your insight statement aloud. Then say "so what?" If you have to pause to think of an answer, the insight isn't fully formed yet. A genuine insight contains its own "so what" — the implication is embedded in the statement, not added as an afterthought.

Putting it together

The theme × participant matrix: the most useful analysis tool no one teaches.

Once your themes are named and your quotes are coded, build a matrix: themes across the top, participants down the side. In each cell, note whether that participant spoke to that theme — and if so, how prominently. This single table reveals more about your data than any other tool.

It shows you which themes are truly cross-cutting (every participant) and which are minority patterns (2–3 participants only). It shows you which participants were anomalies. It shows you whether the study has the evidence to support the claims you're about to make.

Minority patterns: A theme mentioned by only 2 of 12 participants is not a "finding" in the usual sense — but it might be the most important signal in the study. Ask: is this a genuinely unusual perspective, or is it something the majority couldn't articulate because they lacked the language? The matrix helps you spot these and decide consciously how to handle them.

The bottom line

Analysis is not what happens after the research. It is the research.

The words in a transcript are raw material. The insight is something you build from them — through reading, coding, clustering, and the courageous act of saying what the data means. That act is the whole point.

Into the weeds

Software tools for qualitative analysis — and their hidden costs

NVivo, ATLAS.ti, Dedoose, and MAXQDA are the major qualitative data analysis (QDA) software packages. They are powerful, but they carry a hidden cost: the software's structure nudges your analysis toward certain forms. A node-based system like NVivo makes hierarchical themes feel natural — which is fine if your data is hierarchical, and distorting if it isn't.

Many experienced analysts do first-pass coding in a simple text editor or spreadsheet and only move into QDA software when they need to query across a large dataset. The discipline of the craft — noticing, naming, connecting — does not require software. What software helps with is scale: searching for all instances of a code across 40 transcripts, running co-occurrence queries, producing frequency counts.

AI-assisted coding is now possible and useful for first-pass descriptive coding. It is not yet reliable for interpretive coding, where the judgment call about what something means requires contextual understanding that LLMs still handle inconsistently. Use AI for coverage, not for interpretation.

The analyst's positionality problem

Every analyst brings a perspective that shapes what they notice, what they code, and what they name as a theme. This is not a flaw — it's unavoidable — but it needs to be managed. Reflexivity is the practice of naming your own perspective explicitly: what assumptions are you carrying into this analysis? What would you need to see to be surprised?

One practical technique: after completing your own analysis, give a small subset of the data (5–10 quotes) to a colleague who has not read the transcripts and ask them to code independently. Compare codes. Where you diverge is where your analytical perspective is most active — and most in need of examination.

Negative case analysis: the discipline of seeking disconfirmation

Negative case analysis means actively searching for participants or data points that do not fit your emerging theme. If your theme is "users feel anxious about data privacy," look hard for users who don't feel this. If you can't find any, the theme is well-supported. If you find two, those two cases are analytically important — they tell you the conditions under which the theme doesn't apply, which is often the most actionable finding.

Grounded theory, the formal methodology underlying most qual analysis, requires negative case analysis as a check on confirmation bias. Even if you're not doing formal grounded theory, the practice of asking "who doesn't fit this?" before you commit to a theme is one of the most valuable habits an analyst can develop.

Writing findings for non-research audiences

The biggest failure mode in qual reporting is not in the analysis — it's in the translation from analytical language to business language. "Participants exhibited a pattern of passive disengagement marked by absence of negative affect" is not a finding a marketing director can act on. "Customers don't leave angry — they drift away" is the same finding, and it produces a different conversation.

A useful exercise: write every insight twice. Once in analytical language (for your own rigour and for the methodology section). Once in plain English with a concrete example (for the headline and recommendation). The plain English version is the one the client will remember. The analytical version is the one that defends it.

How many quotes is enough evidence for a theme?

There is no universal answer — but there are useful heuristics. A theme mentioned by only one participant is a hypothesis, not a finding. A theme supported by 3+ participants across different demographic or attitudinal segments has reasonable evidential weight. A theme that appears in every session with different language is the strongest possible finding.

What matters is not just frequency but independence. Five participants who all heard each other in a focus group are not independent sources — the group dynamic may have amplified one voice. Twelve IDI participants who each arrived at the same conclusion independently is much stronger evidence. Document how you established the independence of your evidence in your methodology section.

The difference between a finding, a theme, an insight, and a recommendation

Finding: An observation grounded in the data. "Seven of twelve participants mentioned difficulty comparing plans." Findings are descriptive and do not require interpretation.

Theme: A pattern that runs across multiple findings. "Price opacity as a trust-eroding mechanism" — this is interpretive and requires the analyst to have made a judgment about what the findings share.

Insight: A theme plus its implication for the client's specific context. "Customers don't churn on price — they churn when they can't verify that they're getting value, which price opacity prevents." This names a mechanism and its consequence.

Recommendation: An action derived from the insight. "Redesign the plan comparison interface to surface value equivalence at the moment of renewal, not just at acquisition." Recommendations are the analyst's job but should be distinguished clearly from findings — they contain a judgment about what to do, not just what is true.
