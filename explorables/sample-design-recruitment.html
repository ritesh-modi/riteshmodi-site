Where it begins

Your client has a business question. You have to turn it into a list of people.

When a client says "we want to talk to our customers," that sentence contains almost no research information. Customers of what? Loyal ones or recent lapsed? Which age range? Which category usage level? In which region?

Sample design is the discipline of translating vague business intentions into a precise, fieldable specification — a set of rules that tells a recruiter exactly which human beings to go find, and how many of each type.

Picture it Think of your study as a movie with a small cast. Sample design is casting. You decide which roles exist, how many actors fill each role, and what audition criteria someone must pass to get the part. The screener is the audition script. Incidence rate tells you how many people you'll need to audition before you fill the cast.

Building block one

A quota cell is a single slot in your sample — with a name, a number, and a set of rules.

Think of each cell as a seat at the focus group table with a nameplate on it. That nameplate says who is allowed to sit there. Hard quotas mean that seat must be filled — no flexibility. Soft quotas mean you're aiming for a target but you can adjust in the field.

Figure 2 · Quota types and their field implications

Rule of thumb: Attitudinal cells (e.g. "brand loyalists") are hardest to fill because you can't buy a list of attitudes. You must screen everyone to find them — which drives incidence rate down fast.
Picture it A quota cell grid is like a spreadsheet with "must have" cells highlighted in red and "nice to have" cells in yellow. If all your red cells are highly specific — brand-loyal, 45+, female, only uses the product weekly — you've made a very small Venn diagram. The intersection of all those circles is where your recruit lives. The smaller that intersection, the harder the recruit.
"The moment you add a third demographic cut to an attitudinal screener, your incidence rate usually halves."

Building block two

The screener is a funnel, not a form.

Everyone starts as a contact.

You begin with a universe — a list, a panel, a social media ad audience. At this point, you know almost nothing about these people. They're just potential participants.

The first question eliminates the obviously wrong.

The opening screener questions are usually cheap and fast to answer — age, gender, location, category usage. These are your big filters. They drop out the largest chunks of non-qualifiers quickly.

The middle questions get specific.

Product usage frequency, brand familiarity, life stage. These questions are more discriminating. They separate "close but no" from "yes." Each one removes more people from the pool.

The final questions confirm fit — and protect the study.

Industry exclusions (no market researchers!), security questions (nothing too specific about the topic that might bias them), and soft sign-offs. These questions protect research quality, not just compliance.

What remains is your qualified pool.

Everyone who reaches the end without hitting a "terminate" answer is eligible to schedule. The ratio of qualified-to-contacted is your incidence rate — the number that drives everything else.

Say this out loud When you write a screener question, ask yourself: "If I were trying to cheat my way into this study, would this question tell me what the 'right' answer is?" If yes, rewrite it. Screeners should feel like a neutral survey — not like a puzzle with obvious right answers.

Building block three

Incidence rate is the percentage of the population that qualifies — and it controls your budget.

If your incidence rate is 20%, it means 1 in 5 people you contact will qualify. To recruit 40 participants, you need to contact 200. If your IR drops to 5%, you need to contact 800 people to get those same 40 participants. The recruiter charges for every contact, not just the completes.

Picture it Imagine you're fishing. Your incidence rate is how common your target fish is in the lake. A 50% IR means half the fish in that lake are what you want — easy, cheap fishing. A 3% IR means 97% of the fish you pull up are the wrong kind — you're throwing most of them back. The net, the bait, and the boat time all cost the same regardless.

The most common mistake is designing the ideal sample on paper and only calculating the incidence rate later — by which point the budget has already been set. Estimate IR early, and design backwards from what's fieldable.

"Design the sample you can field, not the sample you'd dream of."

The interaction

Quota cells, screener criteria, and incidence rate don't just add up — they multiply.

Here's the danger: each decision feels small on its own. Adding an age band to a quota cell seems harmless. Adding one more screener question seems fine. But these restrictions combine multiplicatively, not additively.

If your target must be: female (≈50%) AND 35–54 (≈30% of all women) AND category user in the last 3 months (≈40% of that group) AND not a market researcher (removes ~3%) AND brand aware (≈60% of remaining) — you're now at roughly 3–4% incidence before you've even asked a single brand-related question.

Picture it Each filter is like passing your target population through a narrower and narrower pipe. The flow rate (incidence) drops every time you add a narrower section. Two wide pipes barely slow the flow. Twelve narrow pipes, stacked, reduce a river to a trickle.

Practical craft

Six decisions that every good sample design has to make deliberately.

Say this out loud — to your client "I can give you a perfectly homogeneous sample that talks to exactly the person you have in mind — but I'd recommend against it. Homogeneity feels safe, but it means every insight comes from the same type of person. A little diversity in the sample produces richer, more credible findings."

The best qualitative sample is not the purest — it's the most strategically constructed. That means making deliberate decisions about variety, about which criteria are load-bearing and which are just habitual, and about where you can afford to be flexible.

The bottom line

Sample design is where the research either earns its credibility — or quietly loses it.

The choices you make before a single participant walks through the door — which cells to fill, how tight the screener, what incidence rate to accept — shape every insight that follows. Make them deliberately.

Into the weeds

How to estimate IR before you field

The fastest method: find a comparable past study, or use the client's own customer data as a reference. If the client knows their NPS, their category penetration, or their brand awareness scores, you can triangulate. In a pinch, run a soft pre-screen with a panel company — a 100-person flash survey costs almost nothing and gives you a real IR estimate before you commit to a recruit budget.

Panel vs. recruiter: which to use and when

Online panels (e.g. Qualtrics, Dynata, Schlesinger) are fast and cheap for high-incidence general consumer studies. But panel fatigue is real — heavy panel members have learned the game and may perform rather than genuinely respond. For niche audiences (C-suite, clinical patients, very specific product users), a specialist recruiter is almost always worth the premium. They build real-world lists rather than sampling from repeat survey-takers.

The "friends and family" problem

When a recruiter is struggling to fill cells, they sometimes lean on referrals — friends of existing participants. This is called snowball sampling and it's fine in some research contexts (ethnographic, community-based), but it introduces social clustering: your sample starts to look like a social network rather than a representative cross-section. Always ask your recruiter if they're using referrals and decide consciously whether it's appropriate for your study.

Sentinel cells: the research design trick

A sentinel cell is a deliberately over-specified quota you use as a "smoke alarm" for recruit difficulty. If you include one very tight cell — say, "heavy users, male, 55+, premium brand only" — and the recruiter can't fill it in week one, you know your overall IR is lower than estimated. The sentinel cell buys you time to renegotiate before the whole recruit falls behind schedule.

How moderators and sample designers should talk to each other

The moderator's discussion guide and the sample design should be created in parallel, not sequentially. If the moderator wants to explore a tension between light and heavy users, the sample better include both. If the guide includes a journaling exercise, the sample should exclude participants who struggle with written expression. This two-way handshake is often skipped — and it's where studies quietly go wrong long before fieldwork begins.
