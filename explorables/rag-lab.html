ACT ONE

A model that reads before it answers

A language model knows what was in its training data and nothing else: not your policies, not your manual, not yesterday's changes. Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) fixes that by finding the right passages in your documents first and handing them to the model with the question.

01 · Why it exists

Three ways to teach a model your documents

Fine-tuning retrains the model on your text. It is slow, costly, has to be repeated whenever a document changes, and the model still cannot tell you where it learned something.

Long context pastes every document into every prompt. It works for a few pages and fails on a few thousand: cost and latency grow with every token, and models attend unevenly to the middle of very long inputs.

Retrieval keeps the documents outside the model, finds the few passages that matter for each question, and sends only those. Updates are instant (re-index the changed file), answers can cite their sources, and cost stays flat as your library grows. The price is a new failure mode: if retrieval misses, the model answers from the wrong text or none.

Misconception: "RAG means vectors." Vectors are one way to retrieve. Keyword search is another, and this page ends with a design that uses no vectors at all.

02 · The whole system

Two phases, many decisions

The build phase runs once per document set: read the documents, cut them into chunks, clean and enrich them, and store them in an index. The query phase runs for every question: find candidate chunks, rerank them, assemble a prompt, generate an answer, and check it.

Every box on the right is a decision with real trade-offs, and every decision changes what the next box receives. You will make each one on your own documents, watch what it does, and measure it with the metrics search engineers use: recall@k, precision@k, MRR and nDCG.

Then you will build a second system, PageIndex, which skips chunking and vectors and lets a model reason its way through your documents' table of contents, and compare the two on the same questions.

Design ruleRetrieval quality caps answer quality. Measure retrieval on its own before you blame the model.

ACT THREE

Cutting documents into pieces

Search returns pieces, not documents. Where you cut decides what can ever be found together.

04 · The concept

Why chunk at all

A whole document is too big to send with every question and too broad to match one well: a long page about returns, delivery and warranty matches every question a little and none of them strongly. A chunk is a span of text small enough to be about one thing. Each chunk is indexed separately, and the best few are sent to the model.

On the right is your first document's own text, line by line. Each coloured band behind the words is one chunk, numbered where it starts; a dark underline marks text that two chunks share (overlap). Scroll and the chunks sweep in from the start. A short document may fit in a single chunk: lower the target size to see where the cuts fall.

05 · Size and overlap

The precision–context trade-off

Small chunks match precisely: a 20-token chunk about delivery times scores high for "how long does delivery take". But they lose context: "Delivery takes 3 to 5 days" means nothing if the sentence saying which orders it applies to landed in another chunk.

Large chunks keep context but dilute the match and cost more tokens in every prompt. Overlap repeats the end of one chunk at the start of the next, so a sentence cut at a boundary still appears whole somewhere: the dark underlines on the right. It costs storage and can send near-duplicates to the model.

06 · What you made

Look at the distribution

The bars are your chunks' sizes in tokens, the dashed line your target. Fixed-size gives a flat wall; by-heading gives wild variation, since sections are as long as their authors wrote them; semantic gives clusters where your text changes topic. Very short chunks are often headings or fragments on their own, and they retrieve badly.

Misconception: "There is a best chunk size." There is a best size for your documents and your questions, and the only way to find it is to measure: Act Seven.
Design ruleCut where the meaning changes, not where a counter runs out, unless you have measured that it doesn't matter.

Try it · step by step

  1. Open the Chunking decision card at the top of this act. Method: Fixed size. Target size: 500 tokens. Overlap: 0%.
  2. Look at the preview. The refund article is 75 words, so it should be one chunk.
  3. Change Target size to 20 tokens. Find where the sentence Items returned after 30 days cannot be refunded falls in the preview.
  4. Change Method to Sentence groups and keep 20 tokens. Look at the same sentence again.

What to expect. At 500 tokens the whole article is one chunk. At 20 tokens the article splits into about four chunks, and the boundary may fall inside a sentence, so one chunk holds the first half of a claim and the next holds the rest. Sentence groups keep whole sentences together, at the cost of uneven chunk sizes.

ACT FOUR

Cleaning and enriching before you store

What you index does not have to be exactly what you show. Clean the noise out; add the context a chunk lost when it was cut.

07 · Normalise

Make equal things look equal

Search compares characters. "ﬁle" with a ligature and "file" are different strings; so are a full-width "５" and "5", and a page footer repeated forty times becomes forty matches for its words. Normalising removes these differences from the index text only: the original text is stored untouched, so citations still quote the document exactly.

08 · Augment

Give every chunk back its context

Cut from its document, "Items returned after 30 days cannot be refunded" no longer says which policy it belongs to. A header (document title › section) puts that back for free. LLM context goes further: DeepSeek reads the whole document and writes a sentence situating each chunk. Generated questions index each chunk under the words users actually ask with, so "send back" can find a passage that only ever says "return".

Design ruleIndex for how people search; store for how documents read.

Try it · step by step

  1. Open Clean and enrich. Set Header to prepend to None. Look at a chunk in the preview.
  2. Set it to Title › section. Look at the same chunk again.
  3. Find the chunk that says Items returned after 30 days cannot be refunded.

What to expect. With None, the chunk is a bare sentence. With Title › section, it begins with the article title. Each article here has one section, so the title and the section look the same; add headings to your own documents to see the difference.

Check yourself. Search for refund after 30 days with each setting. Does the title change which chunk is found?

ACT FIVE

Two ways to find text

Keyword search matches the words. Vector search matches the meaning. Production systems usually use both.

09 · Text and vectors

Stored twice, searched two ways

BM25 scores a chunk by the question's words it contains, weighting rare words more (a match on "receipt" says far more than a match on "the") and penalising long chunks for matching by sheer size. k1 controls how fast repeating a word stops helping; b how hard length is penalised. It is instant, exact and transparent, and it fails on paraphrase.

A vector is a list of numbers an embedding model computes from a text. Texts with similar meanings get similar numbers. DeepSeek does not offer embeddings, so this page runs a small embedding model (all-MiniLM-L6-v2, 384 numbers per chunk) in your browser with WebAssembly, downloaded once when you choose it. The barcode beside each chunk is its vector, one stripe per twelve numbers.

10 · The space of meaning

Close means similar, not relevant

With vectors, every chunk becomes a point. The map squeezes 384 dimensions into two, so it is a sketch, but nearby points really do say similar things. A question becomes a point too, and vector search returns its nearest neighbours.

Comparing a question with every vector is exact and fine for thousands of chunks. HNSW builds a layered graph of neighbours and walks it, checking a fraction of the vectors: that's how search stays fast over millions, and why it occasionally misses the true nearest neighbour. Hybrid runs both searches and fuses the rankings.

Misconception: "The nearest chunk is the answer." Similarity is not relevance: "the warranty does not cover normal wear" sits right next to "the warranty covers faulty parts".
Design ruleStart hybrid: keywords catch codes and names that embeddings blur; embeddings catch the paraphrases keywords miss.

Try it · step by step

  1. Open Index and store. Choose Text (BM25) and press Index. Note the size reported.
  2. Choose Vector, press Index, and note the size again. Then choose Hybrid.
  3. Open the store view. For the vector index, note how many numbers each chunk is stored as.

What to expect. The text index stores words and their positions; its size scales with the vocabulary. The vector index stores one list of numbers per chunk, so its size scales with chunks times dimensions. Hybrid stores both, so it is the largest.

Check yourself. Roughly how many chunks would you need before the vector index is 10 times the size of the text index?

ACT SIX

From your question to the best few passages

Fetch wide and cheap, then judge carefully. Ask something and watch both steps.

11 · Retrieve

Cast a wide net

Retrieval fetches the top k candidates from the index, typically 20 to 50, cheaply. The question can be searched as typed, or transformed first: rewritten into a precise query, turned into a hypothetical answer (HyDE, which looks more like the documents than a question does), or split into sub-questions. The table shows each candidate's keyword score, vector score and fused score.

12 · Rerank

Read each candidate with the question

Retrieval compared the question and each chunk separately. A cross-encoder reads them together, so it notices that "not covered" flips the meaning. It is too slow to run over everything, which is why it only sees the candidates. This one runs in your browser. DeepSeek yes/no asks the model whether each passage answers the question and uses the probability of "yes", read from its log-probabilities, as the score. MMR trades a little relevance for variety, so five near-copies of one paragraph don't fill the prompt.

Scroll: the boxes move from their retrieval rank to their reranked rank.

Design ruleRecall first (fetch enough), precision second (rerank). A reranker cannot rescue a chunk retrieval never fetched.

Try it · step by step

  1. Set Index and store to Text (BM25) and press Index. In Start here, tick only Chunk + index RAG, type money back guarantee and press Ask. Write down the top three passages with their article names.
  2. Change the index to Vector, press Index, and ask money back guarantee again. Write down the top three.
  3. Set Retrieve to Reciprocal rank fusion and ask a third time. Write down the top three.
  4. Turn on Rerank with Cross-encoder and ask again. Note whether the refund article moves.

What to expect. Text (BM25) ranks by the words: the Getting your money back article matches money and back, so it should lead. Vector ranks by meaning: the refund article should sit near the top, though it uses none of the words money or guarantee. Fusion keeps strong results from both. The exact order depends on your chunk settings, so write them down with your answer.

Check yourself. Find one passage that only the vector method returns in its top three. Explain in one sentence why, using the word meaning.

ACT SEVEN

Measuring search, not vibes

Every choice so far was a guess until now. A test set and four numbers turn guesses into decisions.

13 · Ground truth

Questions with their evidence

A test question needs to know which part of your documents answers it. This lab stores that as a character span, the exact sentence, rather than "chunk 12", because chunk 12 is a different piece of text under every chunking method. Spans let you compare fixed-size, by-heading and PageIndex against the same truth.

Write your own (paste the sentence that answers it) or let DeepSeek generate questions with quoted evidence; a quote that is not found word for word in your documents is thrown away rather than trusted. Include questions your documents cannot answer: a good system should find nothing for them.

14 · The four numbers

recall@k, precision@k, MRR, nDCG

recall@k: of the evidence a question needs, how much is in the top k? Low recall means the answer was never handed to the model. precision@k: of the top k, how many are relevant? Low precision means the prompt is full of noise. MRR (mean reciprocal rank): 1 if the first relevant chunk is ranked first, ½ if second, ⅓ if third, averaged over questions; it rewards getting the right thing to the very top. nDCG@k rewards relevant chunks for being high in the list, compared with a perfect ordering.

Misconception: "Precision@5 of 0.2 is terrible." If each question has one relevant span, 0.2 is the best possible. Read precision next to recall, never alone.
Design ruleChange one thing, re-run the same test set, keep what moves recall@k without hurting MRR.

Try it · step by step

  1. In the test-questions panel, ten questions appear with their evidence sentences. Note the last one, Do you offer student discounts?, which has no evidence.
  2. In Evaluate, tick both variants, keep k = 5, leave also answer and judge unticked, and press Evaluate.
  3. Read the table: first relevant at is the rank of the first passage containing the evidence. Read recall@5 and precision@5 for each question.
  4. Set k = 1 and evaluate again. Then set k = 10 and evaluate again.

What to expect. Recall rises as k grows: more chances to include the evidence. Precision usually falls as k grows, because more of what comes back is irrelevant. The student-discount question has no evidence, so its recall reads as not applicable; it tests whether the system stays quiet.

Check yourself. At which k does recall stop improving? Is that a k you could afford in a prompt?

ACT EIGHT

The prompt is part of the system

Retrieval chose the evidence. The prompt decides how the model uses it.

15 · Assemble and answer

Instructions, passages, question

The prompt template wraps the passages in instructions: answer only from these, cite them like [2], and say "I don't know" when they don't contain the answer. That last instruction is what turns a retrieval miss into an honest refusal instead of a confident invention. Edit the template below and ask again; the exact prompt sent is shown under the answer.

Passage order matters with long contexts, where models attend most to the beginning and end. Temperature 0 gives the same answer every time, which you want for support and for evaluation.

Design ruleMake "I don't know" an allowed, rewarded answer, then test it with questions your documents can't answer.

Try it · step by step

  1. In Start here, ask How long until my money is back? with only Chunk + index RAG ticked. Read the answer and its citation numbers.
  2. Open Prompt and answer. Set Passage order to Most relevant first, then ask again.
  3. Set Passage order to Best at both ends, and ask again.
  4. Set Temperature to 0, then to 1.0, and ask again with the first order.

What to expect. The key fact should appear in every version: the money usually appears within 5 to 7 working days after we receive the item. Passage order changes wording and sometimes which passage is cited, not the underlying fact. Temperature 0 gives the same answer each time; temperature 1.0 varies the wording and can drop a detail.

ACT NINE

Is the answer true to its sources?

Good retrieval can still produce a bad answer. Check the answer against the passages it was given.

16 · Faithfulness

Claim by claim

A faithful answer says only what its passages support. The DeepSeek judge splits the answer into atomic claims and checks each one against the passages, so you see which sentence was invented, not just a score. Word overlap is a free proxy that catches copy-paste faithfulness and misses paraphrase.

Faithfulness is not correctness: an answer can faithfully repeat a wrong passage. That's why retrieval is measured separately (Act Seven) and why "I don't know" on unanswerable questions is checked.

Misconception: "An LLM judge is ground truth." It is a second model with its own errors: spot-check its verdicts, and keep the instructions and the model fixed while comparing configurations.

Try it · step by step

  1. In Start here, ask Can I return an item after 60 days for a full refund? with Chunk + index RAG ticked.
  2. Open Judge the answer. Set the judge to Word overlap, then ask the same question again.
  3. Set the judge to DeepSeek claim check and ask again.

What to expect. The refund article says items returned after 30 days cannot be refunded. A correct answer says no. Word overlap rewards answers that reuse passage words, so a fluent wrong answer can score well. The claim check reads each claim and asks whether a passage supports it.

Check yourself. Write a wrong answer that repeats many passage words. Does word overlap still score it highly? Does the claim check?

ACT TEN

Another way: no chunks, no vectors

PageIndex reads your documents the way an expert reads a report: open the contents, pick the chapter, then the section.

17 · The tree

A table of contents as the index

PageIndex, from VectifyAI, replaces the chunk index with a tree: documents, chapters, sections, each node a heading with its span of text and, optionally, a model-written summary. Nothing is cut at arbitrary boundaries and nothing is embedded; a section is retrieved whole.

18 · Navigation by reasoning

Relevance by thinking, not similarity

For each question, a model starts at the root, reads the children's titles and summaries, and chooses where the answer must be, then goes one level deeper, until it reaches sections. With thinking mode on, DeepSeek's reasoning at each step is shown under the path. The idea: similarity finds text that looks like the question; reasoning finds the section that must contain the answer, even when it shares no words with it.

Its costs are the mirror image of vector RAG: no index to build, but a model call per level per question, so it is slower and pricier per query. It depends on good headings, and it returns whole sections.

Design ruleStructured, long documents (reports, manuals, contracts) suit tree navigation; many short, flat documents suit chunk indexes.

Try it · step by step

  1. Open Build the tree and read the heading tree of the five articles.
  2. In Start here, untick everything except PageIndex (tree + reasoning). Ask Can I get my money back if I paid with a voucher?.
  3. Read the path the navigator took, from the document to a section.
  4. Tick only Chunk + index RAG and ask the same question.

What to expect. PageIndex follows the headings to the Getting your money back article, then answers that a voucher is repaid as store credit. The articles here are short with one section each, so the tree is shallow: PageIndex has little to navigate. Chunk RAG finds the same sentence by words and meaning. On short documents the two designs should agree; the difference grows with long, structured documents.

Check yourself. Which design can you audit more easily, and which needs no index build?

ACT ELEVEN

Same questions, two designs

Put both systems through the same test set and let the numbers, and the failures, decide.

19 · Compare

Run both, read the misses

Evaluate both designs with the same k. Then read the per-question tables, not just the averages. Questions that one design finds and the other misses tell you why: a paraphrase the keyword index could not match, a vague heading the navigator could not see past, a fact split across two chunks.

20 · How the choices interact

A decision guide

  • Recall low? Fetch more candidates, go hybrid, add headers or LLM context, or try smaller chunks with overlap.
  • Recall fine, MRR low? The answer is fetched but buried: add a reranker.
  • Precision low and answers rambling? Keep fewer passages after reranking; try MMR.
  • Answers unfaithful with good retrieval? Tighten the prompt; use the stronger model; lower temperature.
  • Long, well-structured documents? Compare PageIndex: it may win on recall at a higher cost per question.
Design ruleEvery stage is a hypothesis. The test set is how you find out which ones were true for your documents.

Try it · step by step

  1. Open Evaluate. Tick both variants, keep k = 5, tick also answer and judge, and press Evaluate. This needs your key.
  2. Read the leaderboard. Find each question where a variant shows not found.
  3. Read the cost and time shown under each answer, then multiply by 10 questions and by 10,000 questions.

What to expect. Leaderboard rows rank the variants on recall, precision and faithfulness. On five short articles the two designs may score alike; the cost difference is what grows. PageIndex usually spends more model tokens per question because the model reads the tree.

Check yourself. Which design would you ship for 10 questions, and which for 10,000? What single number changed your mind?

Before you ship a RAG system

A checklist

  1. Read what your parser produced; fix headings and tables first.
  2. Chunk where meaning changes; measure size and overlap instead of guessing.
  3. Index for how people search (headers, context, questions); store the original for citations.
  4. Start hybrid; add a reranker when recall is fine but the answer is buried.
  5. Keep a test set with span-level evidence, including unanswerable questions, and re-run it after every change.
  6. Make “I don't know” an allowed answer, and check faithfulness claim by claim.
  7. Compare a structure-aware design such as PageIndex on long, well-organised documents.
