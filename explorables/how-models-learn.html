You never see the valley.You only feel the slope.

A model with a billion things to adjust is standing on a hillside in thick fog. It cannot see where the bottom is. It can only feel which way the ground tilts under its feet, take a step that way, and feel again. That is the whole of how models learn — and the rest of this page is that sentence, slowly.

You already know this. This is just the vocabulary.

Nothing new is being introduced here. If you have ever tasted a dish, decided it needed more salt, added some, and tasted again, you have run the entire algorithm. What follows is the field’s names for steps you already understand, plus the honest detail of how a machine does the “adjust it a bit” step when there are a billion things it could adjust.

One word deserves keeping: experiment. Every single step of training is a tiny experiment — change something slightly, measure whether it helped, keep the change if it did. Nobody designs the final numbers inside a model. They are discovered, by experiment, at enormous speed.

Loss: one number for how wrong you are

The loss is the machine’s report card, and it is a single number. Ramesh at the tea stall predicted 120 cups and sold 145 — a gap of 25. Next time, a gap of 7. That shrinking number is the entire point of training.

Why it has to be one number

Suppose someone told you a model was “good on price accuracy, mediocre on new cars, excellent on hatchbacks, poor on diesels”. Now: did the last change help or hurt?

You cannot answer. With four measures pointing in different directions there is no way to say whether a change was an improvement. Boil it down to one number and the question becomes trivial: did the number go down?

Loss is the single dial the entire field is trying to turn down. Every technique, every architecture, every warehouse full of expensive chips exists to make that one number smaller.

Why the errors get squared

The standard loss adds up the errors squared, which sounds like a technicality and is not.

This matches how being wrong actually feels. Pricing a car a little off is a rounding error. Pricing it wildly off loses you the customer and possibly the deal. It is not fifty times worse; it is catastrophically worse. Squaring builds that judgement in.

There is a consequence worth flagging: squaring makes the model obsessive about its worst mistakes, sometimes at the cost of the ordinary ones. That is a choice, and other choices exist. If you would rather the model ignored the occasional wild outlier, you pick a different loss — which is a business decision wearing a technical hat.

Walking downhill in fog

Here is the situation the machine is actually in. You are somewhere on a hillside. It is thick fog. You want to reach the bottom of the valley. You cannot see more than one step in any direction. What do you do?

Almost everyone says the same thing: feel which way the ground slopes under your feet, and take a step that way. Then feel again. Then step again.

That is gradient descent. That is the whole algorithm. Everything else is detail.

Why the fog cannot simply be lifted

Could you not just try every combination and pick the best? Take a small network with 100 adjustable numbers, and allow each just 10 possible values. That is a 1 followed by a hundred zeros of combinations — more than the number of atoms in the observable universe.

Stepping downhill in fog is not a shortcut. It is the only option there has ever been.

Then, feeling the slope

Start at w = 1. Which way is downhill? Wiggle it and look: at w = 0.9 the loss is 42.63; at w = 1.0 it is 38.67; at w = 1.1 it is 34.90. Making w bigger makes the loss smaller. So downhill is up. Take a step up.

One honest note: a real system does not wiggle each weight and re-measure. With a billion weights that would take forever. It uses calculus to get the slope of all of them at once, instantly. But the meaning is exactly the wiggle above. The intuition is not a simplification; it is what the calculus computes.

The one setting that breaks everything

The step size has a name — the learning rate — and it is the most temperamental setting in machine learning. Everyone has lived the analogy: adjusting a shower tap. Turn it too much and you go from freezing to scalding and back, overshooting every time. Turn it too little and you stand there shivering for five minutes. Get it right and you are comfortable in three adjustments.

Below is the same valley and the same starting point. The fog is lifted so you can see what the machine cannot. Drag the step size.

The dotted path is where the walker actually goes. Everything to do with training that people find mysterious — why runs fail, why timelines slip — is largely this one dial being slightly wrong.

Push the dial past about 0.10 and watch what happens. At 0.10 the walk bounces from one side of the valley to the other but still creeps inward. At 0.11 it bounces outward — the loss actively climbs, and the model gets steadily worse the longer you train it.

A one-hundredth of a change flips this from working to broken. Not slightly worse. Broken.

Two things follow, and both matter to anyone paying for this work. Training runs fail for reasons that have nothing to do with your data or your idea — a setting nobody outside the team has heard of was slightly too large. And this is a real part of the cost: “we are tuning hyperparameters” means “we are hunting for numbers like this one”, which is why this kind of project has unpredictable timelines. Nobody can tell you the right learning rate in advance. It is found by experiment.

Who is to blame?

Everything so far had one weight. Real networks have layers of them, and that creates a genuinely hard problem.

A customer sends a dish back. It is bad. Who should change what?

The manager can observe exactly one thing: the customer is unhappy. Nobody handed the chef a note saying “your sauce was 12% over-reduced”. So the blame has to be traced backwards.

the only measurement is at the very end

Each person adjusts their own work in proportion to how much they contributed to the final complaint. Nobody was told the right answer. They were each told their share of the wrongness. That is backpropagation.

The error can only be measured at the end — at the output, where reality gives you an answer. So it has to travel backwards through the layers to reach the parts that caused it. That is why it is called back-propagation.

The hidden middle layers never see the right answer. Not once. They only ever receive their share of the blame from the layer in front of them.

The blame split, in numbers

Two weights in a chain. The input goes through the first, then the second, then out. The target is 6 and the output is 2, so the error is −4. Now share out the blame — and notice that the second weight’s value changes the first weight’s share.

The first weight carries more blame than the second — not because it is more wrong, but because whatever it does gets multiplied by the layer in front of it. Influence, not wrongness.

Why it must run backwards

Look again at how the first weight’s blame was worked out: it needed the second weight’s value in the calculation. You cannot work out the first layer’s blame until you already know the last layer’s. There is no way round it. So the calculation starts at the output — the only place reality speaks — and works its way back to the front, layer by layer.

There is a version of this everyone recognises. A large deal closes after eight meetings, three emails and a dinner. Which one closed it? You only observed the outcome. To apportion credit you have to work backwards through the chain, giving each step a share based on how much it moved things along. That is the same problem, and it is genuinely hard for the same reason.

Why not just solve it directly?

Fair question, and there is a sharp answer. For a straight line, a formula exists. Mathematicians solved that in the 1800s: feed in the data, out comes the exact best answer. No fog, no steps, no learning rate.

For a neural network, no such formula exists and none ever will. Once you stack layers with a “throw away the negatives” step in between, the problem stops having a solution you can write down. There is nothing to solve. You can only walk downhill.

That second-to-last row is the honest bit. A trained network is not the best possible network. It is wherever the walk happened to stop. Run the training again from a different random starting point and you get a different model, with slightly different answers, and no way to say which is truly better.

This is why “we retrained the model and it behaves a bit differently” is a normal sentence in this field, and why reproducibility is genuinely hard. It is worth knowing before it arrives as a surprise.

The two ways it goes wrong

Stopping in the wrong valley

You walk downhill in fog and eventually the ground stops falling away. You have reached a bottom — but is it the bottom? In fog you cannot tell whether the real valley is just over the next ridge.

The everyday version: settling into a job that is comfortable and fine, and never finding out whether the better one existed, because looking would mean climbing.

What is actually done about it: start from several different random places and see whether they agree, and give the walk a bit of momentum so it can roll through a shallow dip rather than stopping in it. Neither is a guarantee. In practice the field has largely made peace with this — a good-enough valley is usually good enough.

Memorising instead of learning

This is the one that costs businesses real money, so it deserves time.

Picture the student who mugs up past papers. Ten years of question papers, memorised perfectly. Scores full marks on every one of them. Then the exam asks something slightly new and they are lost — because they never learned the subject, they learned those papers.

A model does exactly this if you let it. Give it enough weights and it will memorise your training data perfectly — a loss of zero — and be useless on anything it has not seen. Fewer things to learn than examples to learn from is a crude rule, but it is the right instinct.

the one question to ask a vendor“What was the accuracy on data the model had never seen?” If they only quote one accuracy figure, they have not done this — and the number they are quoting is close to meaningless.

The whole loop, and what it does not promise

Make a guess with the weights you have. Measure how wrong it was, as one number. Share the blame backwards through the layers, in proportion to how much each part contributed. Nudge every weight a little way downhill, by an amount set by the step size. Repeat a few million times, until the number stops falling.

That is it. Predict, reconcile, recalibrate, loop — the field just gave the steps longer names.

Two honest limits to carry away. Training does not find the best answer, only a good one it happened to walk into, and a different run finds a different good one. And a low loss is not the same as a useful model — a model that has memorised its training data has a loss of nearly zero and is worthless, which is exactly why the held-back test matters more than the headline number.

Three sentences to keep. Loss is one number, and the whole field exists to make it smaller. The machine never sees the landscape — it only ever feels the slope where it is standing. And the step size is a knife-edge: slightly too large and the model gets worse the longer you train it.
