Deep learning: a building full of clerks who each do one sum

Picture an office where every clerk can do exactly one thing: take a few numbers from the desks below, multiply each by how much they trust that desk, add them up, and write one number on a slip for the floor above. That is not a simplification of a neural network. That is a neural network.

the network on this page is trained in your browser while you read

For most of computing, somebody wrote the instructions down

A woman stands at a conveyor belt in a packing shed, picking up plums and putting them in one of two crates. She has done it for eleven years. Ask her how she decides and she says "you can feel it", and if you push her she gives you something more specific but never quite complete.

The first way to get a machine to take over is to write her decision out as instructions. Something like: if the firmness reading is between 3 and 6.5 and the sugar reading is above 15, ready crate; otherwise the other crate. A set of instructions written by a person and followed exactly by a machine is a rule, and a collection of rules assembled into something a computer runs is a program. Nearly all software ever written works this way and it is an excellent way to build things.

It carries one requirement, and that requirement is the entire story of this page: somebody has to be able to write the rule down.

For plums somebody can. For reading a handwritten postcode off an envelope, nobody can, and not for want of trying. What is the rule separating a 7 from a 1? Something about a horizontal stroke, except many 7s lack one and many 1s have a serif that looks like one. Every rule you write acquires exceptions, every exception needs a rule of its own, and the pile never converges on a program that works.

So the second approach turns the problem round. Instead of writing the rule, you collect examples of the answer and write a procedure that hunts for a rule fitting them. One thing you measured, together with the correct answer for it, is a training example. The study of procedures that find rules from examples is machine learning, which sits inside the older and much broader field of artificial intelligence, the general enterprise of getting machines to do things that seem to require thought.

The particular kind of procedure this page is about builds its rule out of a large number of very simple parts wired together, each doing a little arithmetic and passing the result on. That arrangement is a neural network, named in the 1940s after a loose and now largely abandoned comparison with brain cells. Deep learning is the use of neural networks with many of those parts stacked in sequence, one feeding the next, and the word deep means nothing more exotic than the height of that stack.

Between hand-written rules and deep learning sits a middle era, and what changed across the three is best seen as a list of jobs quietly changing hands.

In the middle era, classical machine learning, a person still decides what to measure and what shape the rule should take, and the machine only hunts for the numbers that go in it. That division works well and still does. Its limit is the very first job on the list: somebody has to decide in advance which measurements will turn out to matter.

For plums that is easy, since firmness and sugar are obviously the relevant things. For a photograph it is not, because the useful measurement is not any individual pixel but something assembled out of thousands of them that nobody can name. People spent years hand-designing measurements for images, with names like SIFT and HOG, and deep learning beat all of them by designing none and letting the search find them.

picture it

Three ways to end up with a cake you like. Write the recipe yourself. Or fix the recipe's structure, flour and sugar and eggs in some proportion, and let a machine try proportions until the cake is right. Or hand a machine a thousand cakes you liked, say nothing about flour or sugar or eggs, and let it work out even that ingredients are the thing to vary.

Each step gives away more of the decision and demands more examples in exchange. That trade is the whole history in one line, and it is why deep learning is so hungry for data.

None of it is free. A hand-written rule can be read, argued with and corrected by anyone in the shed. What deep learning hands back is a large pile of numbers that works and cannot explain itself, and that trade returns at the end of this page.

So: build the smallest possible machine that finds its own rule from examples. The office is the way in.

The whole thing is an office, and every idea in it is a person or a piece of paper

Everything on this page fits in one building, and it is worth walking through the building once, all of it at once, before a single number appears. The eight ideas below are the entire subject. Each one is somebody in the office or something they handle.

Ground floor: the measurements arrive. A plum comes down the belt and two machines read it, one for firmness and one for sugar. Two numbers get written on a slip and the slip goes upstairs. Nothing has been decided yet.

First floor: the clerks. Each clerk on this floor receives a copy of the slip. Each can do exactly one thing. She reads the numbers, multiplies each by a private figure of her own that says how much she cares about that particular measurement, adds the results together, adds one final private number of her own, and writes the total on a fresh slip for the floor above. That clerk is a unit, also called a neuron. The private figures she multiplies by are her weights, one per incoming number, and a weight is exactly a statement of how heavily that input counts and in which direction. The extra number she adds at the end is her bias, and it is how easily she gets excited regardless of what arrives. A whole floor of clerks working in parallel on the same incoming slip is a layer.

Top floor: the manager. One clerk, who never sees the plum. She receives only the slips from the floor below, applies her own weights to those, and writes a single number: how ready this plum is. Producing that number for one new plum is a prediction.

The building itself, wiring and all, is a model: a piece of arithmetic that turns measurements into an answer, containing some numbers that are free to be set. Every one of those free numbers, every weight and every bias, is a parameter. In the small network built later there are thirteen of them.

Downstairs, the complaints desk. Somebody tasted the plum and the machine got it wrong. A complaint is written and sent back up. Every clerk who contributed adjusts her private figures a little, in proportion to how much she contributed to the mistake, and the whole procedure of doing that over and over is training.

That is the complete cast. And it is worth being blunt about what "learning" means here, because the word arrives carrying baggage it has not earned: nothing in the building understands anything. A number that was 0.4 becomes 0.7 because 0.7 made the complaints slightly quieter, and that happens a few million times.

Two things about the office are worth pausing on, because they are the two facts people find hardest to accept about neural networks.

The first is that the wiring never changes. No clerk is ever hired, fired or moved, and no new corridor is ever built. Everything that happens during training is clerks revising their private figures. The building you start with is the building you end with; only the numbers written in the margins are different.

The second is that nobody is in charge and nobody can see the whole problem. The manager never sees a plum. A first-floor clerk never learns whether the final answer was right; she only ever hears that her own contribution should have been a little smaller. There is no plan and no supervisor with an overview, and yet the building as a whole ends up sorting plums correctly.

picture it

A very large kitchen where one person only chops, another only tastes for salt, another only watches the clock. Ask any one of them what the dish is and they cannot tell you. The dish still comes out.

Competence at the level of the building, incompetence at the level of every person in it. That is the deal, and it is also exactly why a trained network cannot explain its decisions: there is nobody inside who knows.

A line here is a rule of the form "multiply firmness by one number, multiply sugar by another, add them, and call it ready if the total clears a threshold". Which, read back slowly, is exactly one clerk from the office: multiply each input by a private figure, add them up, compare against a threshold. One line is one clerk.

A rule built only from multiplying and adding is called linear, and every linear rule on two features draws exactly one straight line across the chart. That line is the decision boundary: the frontier where the answer flips from no to yes.

Twelve is not a guess or a limit of your patience. Every line on this chart was tried, in steps fine enough to catch any distinct answer, roughly six million of them. The best score any of them reaches is twelve out of fourteen.

That is the whole idea and the rest of this page is that idea taken seriously. Before it can be built, one clerk has to be written down precisely enough to run.

One clerk is two multiplications, an addition, and a squash

Here is the entire job description, with nothing left out.

A neuron, which is the clerk from the office, takes some numbers in, multiplies each by a number of its own, adds the results, adds one more number belonging to it alone, and passes the total through a final function. Four steps, all arithmetic.

The numbers it multiplies by are its weights, one per input, and a weight says how much that input matters and in which direction: large and positive means more of this pushes the answer up, negative means it pushes down, near zero means the clerk is ignoring that colleague entirely. The extra number added at the end is the bias, which shifts the result up or down regardless of the inputs and is what lets the clerk put her line somewhere other than through the origin.

total  = (firmness × w₁) + (sugar × w₂) + bias
output = squash(total)

with w₁ = 1, w₂ = 0, bias = −3, a plum of firmness 4.2:
total  = (4.2 × 1) + (18.5 × 0) + (−3) = 1.2
positive, so this clerk says yes: firmness is at least 3

The final step, the squash, is the activation function. It takes the running total, which can be any size, and bends it into a small fixed range so the clerk reports something like a yes-or-no rather than an unbounded number. The simplest is a hard step: negative becomes 0, positive becomes 1. The one used in practice is smooth, and why it has to be smooth gets a chapter of its own shortly.

One clerk is one plank. The shed needs three, and somebody at the gate.

Three clerks ask three questions and the manager combines them

Say the three questions out loud before writing any arithmetic, because the arithmetic will be exactly this and nothing more.

Is it firm enough? Is it not too firm? Is it sweet enough? A plum is ready when all three answers are yes.

Each question is a clerk, so each is a line, so each is three numbers. Written out, with a hard step for the squash:

h₁ firm enough    w = (1, 0)   bias = −3.0  fires when firmness ≥ 3
h₂ not too firm    w = (−1, 0)   bias = 6.5  fires when firmness ≤ 6.5
h₃ sweet enough   w = (0, 1)   bias = −15.0  fires when sugar ≥ 15

out all three       w = (1, 1, 1)   bias = −2.5  fires only when the three add to 3

The manager's inputs are not measurements. They are three answers, each a 0 or a 1, and her bias of −2.5 means the total must reach 3 before she signs off. Two yeses out of three sum to 2 and fall short. Only three clear it. She has never seen a plum in her life.

That floor of question-asking clerks is a hidden layer. Hidden is not mysterious, it is positional: they sit between the measurements coming in and the answer going out, so nothing outside the building ever reads their slips. A stack of layers wired together is a network, and pushing one plum's numbers through it from bottom to top is a forward pass.

This network gets all fourteen plums right. More usefully, the hidden floor says why each rejected plum was rejected.

picture it

Three inspectors on a bench, each holding one gauge and each allowed to say only yes or no. None can pass a plum alone. A supervisor at the end of the bench passes it only if all three said yes.

The supervisor never sees the plum. She sees three yeses and noes and that is enough, because the inspectors have already turned two raw measurements into three useful facts.

That last point is the one to carry forward. The hidden floor's job is not to answer the question. Its job is to turn raw measurements into facts from which the question becomes easy, and here it becomes so easy that a single addition finishes it.

The hidden layer is where the raw measurements stop being measurements and start being reasons.

This building was staffed by hand, by somebody who already knew that ripe means firmness between 3 and 6.5 with sugar over 15. In real work nobody knows. The numbers have to be found from the data, and the hard step, negative becomes 0 and positive becomes 1, is exactly what makes finding them impossible.

The squash has to be smooth, and it has to be there at all

Two separate things go wrong without the right activation function. They are usually run together and neither is obvious, so take them one at a time.

First: without any squash, extra floors are pointless. Take a clerk who multiplies and adds, and feed her output to another clerk who multiplies and adds. Multiplying and adding a multiply-and-add gives you a multiply-and-add. The two floors collapse into one, algebraically, and you are back to a single straight line however tall the building. Twenty floors of pure arithmetic still cannot beat 12 of 14.

An activation function that is not a straight line breaks the collapse, and that property has a name: nonlinearity. It is the reason a network can bend a boundary at all, which makes the squash the load-bearing part rather than a tidying-up step.

Second: with a hard step, nothing can be tuned. Training works by nudging a weight slightly and asking whether things got better. Under a hard step a small nudge almost always changes nothing, because the output is flat at 0 and flat at 1 and then jumps at one exact point. There is never a hint about which way to move. What learning needs is a slope, and a step function has no slope anywhere.

The classic fix is the sigmoid, an S-shaped curve running smoothly from 0 up to 1 and passing through 0.5 when its input is zero. It behaves like a step at the extremes and like a gentle ramp in the middle, and the ramp is what makes learning possible.

The figure below draws two charts side by side, and the second is the point of it. The upper chart is the squash itself: the running total along the bottom, the squashed output up the side. The lower chart plots the slope of that curve at each point, meaning how steeply it is climbing there, and the slope is the only thing a learning procedure can use. A vertical marker sits at the same place on both, so you read an output and its slope together.

picture it

Finding the bottom of a valley in thick fog by feeling the ground with your foot. A hillside tells you which way is down. A perfectly flat floor with a cliff somewhere on it tells you nothing at all: every step feels identical until you fall off.

The sigmoid is a hillside everywhere. That is its whole job, and it is why the clerk trades an honest yes-or-no for a wishy-washy 0.73.

Modern networks mostly use ReLU instead, simpler still: negative becomes 0, positive passes through unchanged. Its slope is exactly 1 across the whole positive half, which matters enormously once buildings get tall, for reasons in the appendix. It is still nonlinear because of the kink at zero, and the kink is enough.

Swap the hard steps in the hand-staffed building for sigmoids and the slips stop reading 0 and 1. They read 0.94 and 0.08, a confidence rather than a verdict, and a threshold at 0.5 turns them back into an answer when one is needed.

Now every clerk responds smoothly to every dial, so a nudge can be evaluated, so the dials can be found by trial and correction rather than by knowing the answer in advance. That needs one more thing: a number saying how wrong the building currently is.

Loss turns how wrong into a single number

This is the complaints desk, made precise.

A building that says 0.94 for a ready plum is doing well. One that says 0.51 is right and barely means it. One that says 0.02 is confidently wrong, which is worse than useless. All of that has to collapse into one number the procedure can try to make smaller, and that number is the loss.

The one used for yes-or-no questions is cross-entropy, and its rule is short: take the probability the building gave to the answer that was actually correct, and score it with the negative natural logarithm of that probability. A logarithm deserves one sentence rather than a section: it is the operation that turns multiplying into adding, and the only consequence that matters here is that halving a probability always adds the same fixed amount to the loss, wherever you started from.

Predict 0.94 on a plum that really was ready and the loss for that plum is 0.06. Predict 0.5 and it is 0.69. Predict 0.02 and it is 3.91. Confident mistakes cost enormously more than hesitant ones, and the penalty runs off to infinity as a prediction approaches certainty in the wrong direction. A building trained on this learns to be unsure rather than boldly wrong.

The loss for the whole batch is the average across all fourteen plums. A building that shrugs and says 0.5 for everything scores exactly 0.693, the natural logarithm of 2, and that is the baseline worth remembering: any loss above it means doing worse than refusing to answer.

The score punishes confidence in proportion to how misplaced it was, which is what stops a building learning to shout.

The untrained network starts at 0.8565, worse than shrugging, because its weights were set to small random numbers and random numbers have no reason to be right. Every step from here tries to make that one number smaller.

And now the real question. There are nine dials on the hidden floor, six weights and three biases, plus three weights and a bias upstairs: thirteen numbers. How does anything work out which way to turn thirteen dials at once, when no clerk can see the complaint and the complainer cannot see the clerks?

Nudge every dial in the direction that lowers the loss

Answer this before reading on. You are at a dial. You do not know where it should be. You are allowed one measurement: how fast the loss changes if you turn it a hair to the right. What do you do?

You turn it the other way, by an amount proportional to how fast the loss was climbing. Then measure again. That is the whole method.

The rate at which the loss changes as one dial turns is that dial's gradient. Collect the gradient for all thirteen dials at once and people call the result a direction in thirteen dimensions, which only means a list of thirteen numbers, one instruction per dial. Nobody can picture that and nobody needs to, because each dial only consults its own number and moves against it. Thirteen dials turned independently is thirteen easy problems, not one impossible one.

Repeating that over and over is gradient descent. One pass in which every plum is shown to the building and every dial is nudged once is an epoch.

The pinned figure has two parts. The large chart is the plums with the region the building currently calls ready shaded in, so watching that shape change is watching it learn. The narrow strip beneath is the loss across all 3000 epochs, with a dot marking the epoch on display. Scroll through it.

Epoch 0: thirteen random numbers

The weights were set by a random number generator, so the boundary is arbitrary and cuts straight through the ready patch. Loss 0.8565, worse than a network that refused to answer. Eight of fourteen correct, which is roughly what guessing gets.

Epoch 20: the boundary finds the sugar edge first

Sugar separates the four sour plums cleanly from everything else, so it is the easiest win available and the gradient points at it hardest. The boundary swings to cut horizontally. Loss is falling fast.

Epoch 50: the region starts to close on the right

With the sour plums handled, the largest remaining contribution to the loss comes from Gale and Holt, sitting sweet and far too firm. A second hidden neuron begins bending the boundary in to shut them out. Loss 0.1060, and all fourteen are now on the correct side.

Epoch 200: the patch is enclosed

The third edge arrives and Isla and Juno fall outside on the soft left. What has appeared is the same three-sided region that was built by hand, except nobody told it that firmness matters twice or that the thresholds are 3 and 6.5. Loss 0.0142.

Epoch 800: the same shape, held more firmly

The boundary barely moves now. What changes is confidence: predictions that were 0.8 become 0.97, and the loss keeps falling because cross-entropy still pays for hesitation. Loss 0.0029.

Epoch 3000: 0.0007, and nothing left to learn here

The curve has flattened. Every plum is on the right side with high confidence, and further epochs buy fractions of a percent. Whether that is a finished model or a memorised one is a question this data cannot answer, and it is coming.

picture it

A shower with two taps and no labels, in the dark. You cannot see the plumbing. Nudge one tap, feel whether the water got closer to right, nudge again. Then the other. Round and round.

You will find a decent temperature within a minute, and you will never learn anything about the plumbing, which is exactly the deal a trained network offers. Thirteen numbers that work and no explanation of itself, which is the second office fact arriving as arithmetic.

Two honest cautions about that picture. Gradient descent finds a low place, not necessarily the lowest, so a run starting from different random numbers can settle somewhere slightly different. And the size of each nudge is a choice rather than a law, and it turns out to be the single most consequential number in the procedure.

The learning rate decides whether it learns, crawls, or explodes

Each dial moves by its gradient multiplied by one shared number. That number is the learning rate, and a person chooses it before training starts rather than the building working it out, which makes it a hyperparameter: a knob on the learning procedure itself, sitting outside everything the procedure adjusts.

In office terms it is how dramatically every clerk overreacts to a complaint. Set it tiny and each clerk shifts her figures by a hair, so a thousand complaints later nothing much has changed. Set it enormous and one complaint sends every clerk lurching so far in the opposite direction that the next plum is wrong in a new and worse way, which produces another complaint, which produces another lurch.

Drag it. Everything else is held fixed: same fourteen plums, same three clerks, same random starting numbers, same 400 epochs. Only the size of each nudge changes.

At 0.005 the loss ends at 0.7021 and the building gets seven of fourteen, which is what a coin gets. It is not broken. It is moving correctly, in steps so small that 400 epochs cover almost no ground.

At 0.5 it lands on 0.0665 and all fourteen. At 3 it reaches 0.0062, faster still. Somewhere between those is the comfortable range, and notice how wide it is: a factor of six, and both ends work.

At 30 the loss jumps to 5.0 partway through before struggling back to 0.3323, finishing worse than the run at 0.5. At 150 it climbs to 15.8 and stays broken. The step is now so large that each nudge overshoots the bottom and lands further up the far side than it started, so every correction makes the error bigger. That runaway is divergence, and it is the loudest failure in the procedure because the loss climbs while the machine does exactly what it was told.

picture it

Reversing into a parking space using only mirrors. Move six inches at a time and you get there eventually, long after everyone has left. Move six feet at a time and you cross the space on every attempt, hit the kerb on the far side, and correct harder each time.

Nothing was wrong with your judgement of which way to go. The direction was right every single time. Only the size of the move was wrong, and that alone is enough to make the whole thing fail.

Nobody leaves it fixed in practice. Real training usually starts at a workable rate and shrinks it over time, on the reasoning that big steps help while you are far away and hurt once you are close. An optimiser is the piece of code that decides how a gradient becomes an actual step; the most widely used, Adam, keeps a separate effective step size for every dial and adjusts each from that dial's own recent history, so a dial under steady pressure speeds up while one being pushed back and forth slows down.

All of this assumed each dial's gradient is simply available. For the manager's weights that is nearly true. For a weight buried on the hidden floor, which affects the loss only through everything that happens above it, working it out is a real problem, and solving it is what made tall buildings possible.

Blame flows back down the building, one floor at a time

Pick one weight: the figure the second hidden clerk applies to firmness. Nudge it up slightly. What happens to the loss?

Follow it up the building. A bigger weight raises that clerk's total, which moves her sigmoid output by an amount depending on how steep the sigmoid is right there, which changes what the manager receives, multiplied by the weight the manager applies to her, which moves the final prediction, which changes the loss by an amount depending on how far the prediction was from the label.

Five steps, and every one is a rate: how much this moves per unit of that. The rule for chaining rates is that you multiply them, which is the chain rule, and it is the only piece of calculus this page needs. It is worth seeing why rather than taking it on trust. Say nudging the weight by one unit moves the clerk's total by 2, and one unit of movement in that total moves her output by 0.2. Then one unit of movement in the weight moves the output by 2 × 0.2, which is 0.4. Rates chain by multiplying for the same reason that miles per hour times hours gives miles.

Doing that separately for every weight would repeat enormous amounts of work, since weights on the same floor share almost all of their path. Backpropagation is the observation that you can compute it once per floor instead, working downwards from the top: each floor receives a number saying how much the loss changes per unit of its own output, uses it to finish the gradients for its own weights, and passes a suitably adjusted number down to the floor below. One downward sweep, every gradient in the building.

picture it

A dish comes back to the kitchen too salty. The head chef does not test every ingredient in the building. He tells the sauce station the sauce was too salty; the sauce station knows the stock is most of the sauce and tells the stock pot, adjusted for how much stock the sauce actually contained; the stock pot tells whoever salted it.

Each station only has to know its own contribution and pass a corrected message one step back. Nobody ever holds the whole chain, and the message arriving at the salt is exactly as strong as the salt deserved. That is the complaint from the office, working properly.

One detail there is load-bearing. Each message gets multiplied by a rate on the way down, and sigmoid slopes are never larger than 0.25. Chain twenty of those and the message reaching the ground floor has been multiplied by about a trillionth. The lowest floors stop receiving a usable signal and stop learning, which is the vanishing gradient problem, and it is why buildings stayed short for decades.

ReLU is the main part of the fix, because its slope on the positive side is exactly 1 and multiplying by 1 twenty times changes nothing. That, plus better initial values and shortcut corridors letting the message skip floors, is what turned a two-floor curiosity into buildings hundreds of floors tall. The word deep refers to nothing more exotic than that: many hidden floors, made trainable.

So the machinery works, and the plum building reaches a loss of 0.0007 on its fourteen plums. Which raises a question those fourteen plums are structurally incapable of answering.

A building graded on the plums it studied will always look brilliant

A loss of 0.0007 on fourteen plums proves one thing: the building can reproduce fourteen answers it was shown fourteen thousand times. That is a claim about memory. The shed needs a claim about tomorrow's fruit.

The fix is to hide some data from it. Split the rows into a training set, which is what the dials get nudged on, and a test set, which the building never sees during training and is scored on afterwards. The test score estimates how it will do on plums nobody has tasted, and that ability is called generalisation.

Fourteen rows is far too few to split, so this section uses 120 plums generated from the same ripeness rule, with 12 percent of the labels flipped on purpose, because real tasters disagree and real probes drift. Eighty go in the training set and forty are held back. Two buildings then train on the identical eighty rows: one with three clerks on the hidden floor, one with forty.

The figure below plots two loss curves against epoch. The solid dark line is loss on the eighty plums the building studies; the dashed red line is loss on the forty it has never seen. Those two moving together means learning. The dashed one turning upward while the solid one keeps falling is the failure this section is about.

The small building ends at a training loss of 0.4196 and a test loss of 0.3279, scoring 90 percent on the held-out plums. It never fits the training data especially well, and that is the point: with three clerks it can only draw a three-sided region, so it has no way to bend around the mislabelled ones.

The large one drives its training loss down to 0.1903 and its training accuracy up to 91.3 percent, and its test loss rises, from a low of 0.3687 around epoch 300 to 0.5891 by the end. Test accuracy drops from 90 percent to 80. It got better at the eighty plums by getting worse at plums in general.

That is overfitting: enough freedom to bend the boundary around individual training rows, so it starts fitting the 12 percent of labels that were flipped noise. Those flips are not a pattern. There is nothing there to learn, and forty clerks are perfectly capable of learning it anyway.

picture it

Two students revising from the same past paper. One learns why the answers are what they are. The other memorises the answer sheet, including the two questions where the sheet was printed wrong.

On that paper the second student scores higher. The mistakes on the answer sheet are exactly what the memoriser learns hardest, because they are the part that cannot be got by understanding, and the part that will never appear again.

The rising dashed line also tells you when to stop. Training past the point where test loss bottoms out spends compute to get worse, and stopping there is early stopping: the cheapest of the standard defences, alongside a smaller building, more data, and regularisation methods such as dropout and weight decay, which are in the appendix.

Ninety percent on held-out fruit sounds like a machine worth installing. It is not, and the reason is invisible in that number.

Ninety percent accurate, and it misses half the ripe plums

Take the small building's forty held-out plums and sort its answers into four boxes: said ready and was right, said ready and was wrong, said not ready and was wrong, said not ready and was right. That four-box table is a confusion matrix, and it is the only honest way to look at a yes-or-no model.

Here is what it holds. Four plums it correctly called ready. Zero it wrongly called ready. Four ready plums it wrongly rejected. Thirty-two it correctly rejected.

Thirty-six of forty right is 90 percent, which is accuracy: the share of all answers that were correct. Now look at what accuracy hides. Only eight of those forty plums were ready in the first place, so a machine switched off entirely, saying no to everything, scores 32 out of 40, which is 80 percent. That number, the score of the laziest possible rule, is the baseline, and it is the only thing that makes an accuracy figure interpretable at all. Our 90 percent bought ten points over doing nothing.

Two sharper numbers pull the boxes apart. Precision asks: of the plums it called ready, what fraction really were? Four of four, so 1.000. Recall asks: of the plums that really were ready, what fraction did it find? Four of eight, so 0.500. Everything it ships is good. It throws away half the crop.

Green boxes are the two kinds of correct answer and red boxes the two kinds of mistake. Three bars underneath turn the counts into scores, and a dashed marker across them sits at 0.800, the score of a machine that always says no.

Drag the threshold. The network's output is a number between 0 and 1; the threshold is where you cut it into yes and no. Lowering it moves plums out of the missed box and into the found box, and some of them into the wrongly-shipped box.

Drag the threshold down and recall climbs while precision falls, because a looser cut catches more ready plums and more unready ones with them. Drag it up and the reverse. No setting maximises both, and that is not a defect of this model. It is the trade being made, and the threshold is where a person decides which mistake is cheaper.

picture it

A smoke alarm. Set it sensitive and it catches every fire and also every piece of toast, until somebody takes the battery out. Set it dull and it never nags and one night it stays quiet.

No setting is simply correct, only a judgement about which failure you would rather live with, and that judgement belongs to whoever lives in the house rather than to the alarm.

For this shed the answer is probably clear: a rejected ripe plum loses the revenue on one plum, while a shipped unripe plum loses a customer. That argues for keeping precision high and living with the misses, which is what the 0.5 threshold already does. The point is not that 0.5 was wrong. It is that nobody chose it, and 90 percent accuracy would never have prompted anyone to look.

Where accuracy misleads worst. The rarer the thing you are hunting, the more useless it gets. On a task where one row in a thousand is fraud, a model saying no to everything is 99.9 percent accurate and completely worthless. Print the baseline next to the accuracy every time, and look at recall for the class you actually care about.

All of this ran on three clerks on one floor. Nothing yet explains what a second floor would buy, which leaves the word "deep" still doing no work.

Depth means facts built out of facts

The hidden floor turned two measurements into three facts: firm enough, not too firm, sweet enough. The manager then worked on those facts rather than on the measurements.

Add a second hidden floor and the same thing happens one level up. Its clerks take the three facts as inputs and build facts about facts: "right texture", meaning firm enough and not too firm at once, is a single clerk on the second floor combining two from the first. That is depth, and the ladder of increasingly assembled facts is a feature hierarchy.

On plums this is overkill and it is worth saying so plainly rather than pretending otherwise: two features and one simple region need one hidden floor, and a second adds capacity with nothing to do. Depth earns its keep when the raw inputs sit far below the level the question is asked at.

A photograph is the standard case. The inputs are a million brightness numbers and the question is whether there is a dog. No weighted sum of pixel brightnesses answers that. But early floors can detect edges from neighbouring pixels, later ones assemble edges into corners and textures, later ones assemble those into eyes and fur and paws, and only near the top does anything resemble the question. Nobody designs that ladder. It is what gradient descent produces when a tall enough building is trained on enough labelled photographs.

picture it

Reading. Your eye does not go from ink to meaning in one jump. Strokes become letters, letters become words, words become a sentence, and only then does anything mean anything. Ask someone to identify the letter "e" and they can; ask which retinal cells fired and they cannot, because that level was consumed on the way up.

Each level is built only from the level below it, and no level had to be designed by whoever learned to read.

One caution goes with the word "deep". A building with a single hidden floor, given enough clerks on it, can already approximate essentially any boundary you like: that is a proved result, the universal approximation theorem. Depth is not about what is possible. It is about what is reachable with a sane number of clerks and a sane amount of data, and on that practical measure it wins overwhelmingly, which is why every model anybody has heard of is deep rather than merely wide.

Every building on this page has had the same floor plan: everybody on one floor wired to everybody on the next. That is a starting point rather than the only option, and the interesting families of network are the ones that change it.

The floor plan is a guess about the shape of the data

The plum building wires every input to every clerk on the hidden floor. That arrangement is called fully connected, and choosing it was a statement: any measurement might matter to any question, and nothing about their order or position means anything. For two probe readings that statement is true.

An architecture is the pattern of who is wired to whom, and the good ones are the ones whose statement matches the data. Three matter most.

Convolutional networks are for data where position means something and a pattern means the same thing anywhere in it, which describes images exactly. Instead of one weight per pixel, a small patch of weights, a filter, slides across the whole image and reports what it found at each spot. That change is convolution, and it buys two things: an edge detector learned in one corner works in every corner, and nine weights replace a million, which is what makes training on photographs feasible at all. In office terms, one clerk with a magnifying glass sweeping the page instead of a million clerks each staring at one dot.

Recurrent networks are for data arriving in order with no fixed length, like speech or a sensor trace. The building reads one item at a time and carries a running summary of everything so far into the next step, and that loop back into itself is recurrence. The weakness is that the summary is a fixed-size bottleneck, so early items fade: a whisper passed down a long line.

Transformers replaced them for most sequence work by dropping the loop entirely. Every position looks at every other position at once and decides, from content rather than distance, which ones matter to it. That mechanism is attention, and because nothing waits for anything else, the whole sequence trains in parallel, which is the practical reason these models could be scaled to the size they now are. They are what sits underneath the language models you have used.

Into the weeds

Why standardise the inputs before training?

Firmness runs 0 to 10 and sugar runs 8 to 22, so a weight on sugar starts out pulling roughly twice as hard on the total. The gradients come out lopsided and the useful learning rate for one weight is wrong for the other. Subtracting each feature's mean and dividing by its standard deviation, which for these plums is mean 5.064 and 16.064 with spreads 2.205 and 3.273, puts both on the same footing. Every network on this page trains on standardised inputs and converts back only for drawing.

Batches, stochastic gradient descent, and what an epoch really costs

Everything here computes the gradient over all the training rows before taking one step, which is full-batch gradient descent and is only affordable because there are 14 or 80 rows. At realistic scale you take a small random sample, a mini-batch of perhaps 32 or 256 rows, and step on that. The gradient is noisier, the steps are vastly cheaper, and the noise turns out to help by shaking the run out of poor local minima. An epoch then means one pass through the data in many small steps rather than one big one.

Dropout and weight decay

Two standard defences against the overfitting seen with 40 neurons. Dropout switches off a random fraction of neurons on every training step, so no neuron can rely on any particular other one being present, which stops the network building fragile specialised detectors for individual training rows. Weight decay adds a penalty proportional to the size of the weights, so a weight has to earn its magnitude by reducing the loss more than the penalty costs. Both work by making it harder to fit noise without making it much harder to fit signal.

Where the initial weights come from

Not zero: if every neuron in a layer starts identical it receives an identical gradient forever and the layer never becomes more than one neuron. Not large either, because a sigmoid fed a large total sits flat at 0 or 1 where its slope is nearly zero and no gradient flows. The standard schemes, Xavier for sigmoid or tanh and He for ReLU, pick a random scale from the number of connections into and out of the layer so the signal keeps roughly the same size as it crosses each one.

Softmax, for more than two answers

Sigmoid handles yes-or-no. For a choice among several classes you put one output neuron per class and pass them through softmax, which exponentiates each total and divides by the sum of them all, giving numbers that are positive and add to 1. Cross-entropy generalises with no change in spirit: take the negative log of the probability assigned to the correct class.

Why the loss surface is not a simple bowl

Swapping any two hidden neurons, along with their weights, gives a different set of numbers describing the identical network, so every solution has an enormous number of exact twins and the surface is full of symmetric copies. For a long time the fear was getting trapped in poor local minima; the current understanding is that in high dimensions the real obstacles are saddle points, places flat in some directions and downhill in others, and that most local minima found in practice are about as good as each other.

The vanishing gradient, with the numbers

The sigmoid's slope is at most 0.25, at its centre, and much less anywhere else. Backpropagation multiplies one such slope in per layer, so ten layers scale the message by at most 0.25 to the tenth, roughly one in a million, and twenty layers by roughly one in a trillion. The first layers receive nothing usable. ReLU's slope of exactly 1 on the positive side removes the shrinkage, and residual connections that add a layer's input to its output give the gradient a path that skips the multiplication entirely.

What a learning rate schedule looks like

A fixed rate is a compromise between covering ground early and settling precisely later. Schedules resolve it by changing the rate over training: step decay cuts it by a factor at fixed epochs, cosine annealing eases it smoothly toward zero, and a warmup ramps it up over the first few hundred steps to stop a large early gradient from wrecking freshly initialised weights. Large model training uses warmup followed by cosine decay almost universally.

How the 12-of-14 limit was established

By exhaustive search rather than by argument. Every straight boundary is described by three numbers, two coefficients and an offset, and both classifications of each side were scored. Sweeping the coefficients across the range that produces any distinct answer on this data, at a resolution fine enough that no distinct grouping is skipped, gives roughly six million lines. The best of them separates twelve of the fourteen plums. Any finer sweep changes nothing, because fourteen points admit only finitely many distinct linear splits.

Is any of this like a brain?

The vocabulary came from a 1943 attempt to model neurons and the name stuck, which causes more confusion than it is worth. A biological neuron communicates in spike timings, has thousands of connections with their own chemistry, and nobody has shown that brains do anything resembling backpropagation, which needs an exact backward copy of every forward connection. The useful framing is that this is curve fitting with many parameters, done well, and the resemblance to biology is a historical accident of naming.

Every network on this page is trained in your browser by gradient descent with backpropagation written out in plain JavaScript. Losses, accuracies, decision boundaries, the confusion matrix and the learning rate curves are all measured from those runs on each interaction, not stored.

The fourteen plums are invented, and chosen so that the ready ones form a region no straight line can enclose. The overfitting and evaluation sections use 120 further plums generated from the same rule with 12 percent of labels flipped, split 80 for training and 40 held back. Backpropagation as applied to networks is from Rumelhart, Hinton and Williams, 1986. ReLU's role in deep networks is from Glorot, Bordes and Bengio, 2011. The universal approximation result is Cybenko, 1989. Attention is from Vaswani and colleagues, 2017.

Firmness and Brix are real measurements taken in real packing sheds. The specific thresholds here are not; they were picked to make the mechanism visible. The office is a teaching device rather than a claim about how anything is implemented: there are no clerks, only arithmetic.
