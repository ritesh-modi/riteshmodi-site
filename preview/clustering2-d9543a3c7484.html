Clustering: drawing constellations in data

Everybody who has ever looked at a night sky has done this. Thousands of scattered points, nobody handing out names, and yet your eye insists that those few belong together. Clustering is that instinct written down carefully enough that a machine can follow it.

every grouping on this page is computed live, not stored

Nobody was ever told where the constellations are

Go outside on a clear night, away from town, and look up. There are a few thousand stars visible, scattered across the whole sky, and not one of them arrived with a name attached or a line drawn to its neighbours.

And yet within a few seconds you will notice that some of them go together. Seven bright ones in a long-handled shape. A tight little knot in one corner. Three in a perfect short row. You did not decide to look for those. Your eye found them because they were closer together than the stars around them, and that closeness felt like it meant something.

Every culture that ever looked up did the same thing and wrote down the results. The Greeks drew a hunter. Looking at exactly the same stars, the Chinese drew a completely different set of shapes, and so did the Polynesians, and the Lakota. They were all looking at the identical sky. They disagreed, and none of them was wrong.

Hold onto that, because it is the strangest and most important fact about the subject: the groups were not in the sky. They were in the looking. There is no sealed envelope anywhere containing the correct constellations, which means no drawing can be checked against it, which means the only question you can ever ask of a grouping is whether it is useful to somebody. And they were extremely useful. They told sailors where north was and told farmers when to plant.

Now the vocabulary, because four words describe what just happened and they get used constantly from here on.

A category is a named group that things can belong to, like "the Plough". A label is a category attached to one specific thing, so writing "Plough" against a particular star is giving that star a label. An answer key is a complete list of the correct labels, written down in advance by someone who knew. And the entire difference between the two halves of this field is whether such a key exists.

When it does exist, the task is supervised: someone stood over the process holding the correct answers, and a machine can learn to reproduce them and be graded on how often it succeeds. Teaching a machine to recognise handwritten digits is supervised, because for every scribble in the training pile a person wrote down which digit it really was.

When no key exists, the task is unsupervised, and grouping things under those conditions is called clustering. One of the groups it produces is a cluster. The sky is the pure case: measurements available in abundance, categories entirely absent, and the whole job is to propose some.

picture it

Two people tidying the same drawer of odds and ends. One sorts by material: metal here, plastic there. The other sorts by what they are for: things for the bike, things for the kitchen. They end up with different piles from the same objects.

Ask which one is right and the question dissolves. Ask which one makes it easier to find a bike spanner on a wet morning, and the question has an answer. That is the only kind of answer clustering ever gives.

Before this instinct can be handed to a machine, there is a middle step worth naming, because it is what people did for a very long time and it is what clustering replaces.

They wrote the rule down. Rather than looking at the sky and noticing, an astronomer would declare a boundary: everything brighter than the fourth magnitude in this patch of sky belongs to this constellation. That is a hand-written rule, and it works exactly as long as somebody already knows what the groups are and can describe the line between them. For the sky they did know, because the groups were traditional and inherited.

For a shop with three thousand customers, nobody knows. That is the situation clustering was built for, and the rest of this page builds the machinery from scratch. It starts by asking what "those belong together" could possibly mean to something that cannot see.

Three questions stand between the instinct and a method

Your eye did the whole job in half a second. To hand it over, that half second has to be broken into pieces small enough that each one is arithmetic. It comes apart into exactly three questions, and every clustering method in existence is a set of three answers to them.

The first question: what does alike mean? For stars it meant close together on the sky, which is convenient because closeness is already a distance you could measure with a ruler. For customers there is no sky. There are columns of numbers, and one of them might be how often somebody visits and another how much they spend. Each such column is a feature, meaning one measurement recorded for everybody. The trick, and it is the central trick of the whole field, is to treat each feature as a direction and each thing as a position in that space, so that the row becomes a data point and "alike" becomes an actual measurable distance again.

The second question: where is a group? A single star has a position. Seven stars have seven positions, and the group as a whole needs one, otherwise there is nothing to compare a new star against. The usual answer is to average them: the balance point of the group, called its centre. It is not one of the members and usually sits where nothing is.

The third question: how many groups are there? This is the one people expect a machine to answer and it will not. The Greeks drew 48 constellations; modern astronomers recognise 88; you could equally draw 3 or 300 in the same sky. Nothing in the stars says which. As you will see, this question stays yours.

Answer those three and you have a clustering method. The rest of this page answers them one at a time, on data small enough that every number can be printed on the page.

Now for something concrete and small enough to hold in your head at once.

Twelve regulars in a bakery, which is a sky with twelve stars

Sam runs a small bakery and has twelve people who come in often enough that she recognises them. She wants to treat different kinds of regular differently, and she does not know what the kinds are. That is the sky problem with the poetry removed.

Over one month she recorded three things about each of them: how many times they came in, what they spent on an average visit, and what share of their order was pastry rather than bread or coffee. Nobody wrote down what kind of customer anybody is, because that is the thing she is trying to find out.

Two words describe the shape of this and both were introduced a moment ago in the abstract. Here they are concretely. A row is everything recorded about one person: Ana is a row. A feature is one measurement recorded for everybody: visits is a feature, spend is a feature. Twelve rows, three features.

Visits runs along the bottom, spend runs up the side. Ana came 22 times and spent £3.40 a visit, so she lands far to the right and very near the floor. picture it

Twelve index cards, each with a name and some numbers. Shuffle them all day and you learn nothing. So clear a table, call the left edge "never comes in" and the right edge "comes in daily", call the near edge "spends little" and the far edge "spends a lot", and lay each card where its numbers say.

You have not changed a single number. You have only made distance mean something, and that is the entire trick from the first question.

Now look at where the cards landed, and notice that you are doing exactly what you did with the stars. They are not spread evenly. There is a knot hugging the bottom right, another up in the top left, and a small huddle in the middle. Your eye found that instantly and nobody told it the names.

Sam wants to act on those three groups. So the obvious first move is to draw a line and split them, which is what the astronomer with the magnitude threshold did. It fails here, and how it fails is the reason the rest of this page exists.

The line you draw by hand breaks on the next customer

Try the hand-written rule first, because it fails instructively.

Sam decides that anyone coming in more than twelve times a month is a "regular" and everyone else is an "occasional". One number, one line, done. That twelve is a rule: a threshold picked by a person, applied to one feature, that hands out labels. She is inventing both the categories and the boundary, in advance, which is precisely the thing she was unable to do.

In the figure below, the heavy vertical line is Sam's threshold and the two tinted regions are the two labels it hands out. There is no good place to put it. Slide it left and the middle huddle gets called regulars alongside Ana, who visits four times a week, which they plainly are not. Slide it right and they get filed with Hana, who comes three times a month and spends twenty-six pounds. picture it

Sorting a drawer of screws with only a ruler: everything under four centimetres left, everything over it right. It works until you notice half the short ones are wood screws and half are machine screws, and length was never going to tell you that.

The measurement you happened to threshold is not the thing that made the groups. Back in the sky, this is the astronomer who declares a brightness cutoff and finds it slices straight through the middle of a shape everybody can see.

Sam could add a rule for spend, then one for pastry share, and keep going. That road ends in a pile of hand-tuned numbers chosen by eye, which nobody can justify and which all need revisiting when a thirteenth customer walks in.

The deeper problem is not clumsiness. She is being asked to state the answer before she has it. So: back to the first question. What could "alike" mean to something that cannot see the chart?

Clustering is grouping when there is no answer key

The name and the definition were given in the sky chapter. Now that the need has been felt on real data, they are worth restating precisely and then contrasting with the alternative, because the contrast is what makes the definition bite.

Clustering splits rows into groups that were never written down anywhere, using only how alike the rows are. No target column, no correct answer, nothing to check against.

Now suppose Sam had a fourth column, filled in by hand, saying "commuter" or "weekend family" or "lunch crowd" for each customer. She could feed a program the measurements alongside those answers and have it learn to predict the label for somebody it has never seen. That is supervised learning, and the word refers exactly to the presence of that column: someone stood over the process holding the answers. Clustering runs with that column missing, which is why it is unsupervised. Nobody holds the answers because nobody has them.

The figure below puts the same twelve customers on the left and the group column on the right. When the answer column is empty, each cell is drawn as a dashed empty box, which is the visual form of "nobody wrote this down". When it is filled, each customer's square takes the colour of their group and the name appears in the column.

picture it

A teacher marking exams has a mark scheme: every answer is right or wrong against something already written down. That is supervised.

A naturalist emptying a bag of shells onto a bench has no mark scheme. She sorts by shape and ridging until piles form, then goes looking for names to put on them. The piles come first and the names come second, which is exactly backwards from the exam, and exactly what the constellation-drawers were doing.

Two consequences follow and both matter later. No clustering result is ever right or wrong the way a marked exam is; it can only be more or less useful, which is the constellation lesson arriving as a technical constraint. And because there is nothing to check against, the method needs its own internal idea of what a good grouping looks like. Every clustering algorithm is essentially one answer to that.

All of them rest on a word that has been doing quiet work in every sentence so far. Before anything can be grouped, alike has to become a number.

Alike is a distance, and distance is arithmetic

Point at Ana and Ben on the chart and say why they are alike, in a way a machine could check.

"They are near each other" is right, and it is already the answer, because near has a definition. Ana sits at 22 visits and £3.40. Ben sits at 19 and £4.10. Go along 3 visits, then up 0.70 pounds, and you have walked two sides of a right-angled triangle. The straight line between them is the third side, and its length comes from the rule about right-angled triangles you were taught at school.

distance(Ana, Ben)
  = √( (22 − 19)² + (3.40 − 4.10)² )
  = √( 9.00 + 0.49 )
  = √9.49 = 3.08
distance(Ana, Hana) is 29.83, which is why nobody is tempted to group them

That is Euclidean distance: subtract the rows feature by feature, square each difference, add the squares, take the square root. The squaring is worth two sentences because the formula is easy to memorise and easy not to understand. It removes the minus signs, so the order of subtraction stops mattering. And it makes big gaps count for far more than small ones: one gap of 10 contributes 100, while ten separate gaps of 1 contribute 10 in total.

The figure below draws the triangle between any two customers you pick. The two dashed legs are the two feature differences, one horizontal and one vertical, and the solid sloped side is the distance itself. The numbers printed beside the legs are the actual differences, and the length of the sloped side is computed rather than drawn to look convincing.

picture it

Two pins in a paper map. You do not need to know what the map shows to measure between them: lay a ruler across and read the number. Squaring and square-rooting is what a computer does instead of owning a ruler.

Every extra feature is one more direction the pins can be apart in, and the formula does not care how many there are. Three features adds a third squared term inside the root; three hundred adds three hundred. Nobody can picture that and nobody has to, because the arithmetic never changes. This is the moment the sky stops being a metaphor: with two features you are literally looking at a sky, and with fifty you are doing the same arithmetic in a room you cannot enter.

Two things follow at once. Alike now has a number, so groupings can be judged rather than admired. And because the formula treats every feature identically, a feature whose numbers happen to be large will produce large squared differences and quietly dominate everything. Hold onto that; it comes back to bite in a later chapter.

That answers the first question. The second is harder, because distance compares two things and a group has four. Where is a group?

A group's position is the average of its members

Answer fast: if Ivo, Jo, Kit and Lena are one group, where is that group?

The useful answer is the plain one. Add their visits and divide by four. Add their spend and divide by four. That pair of averages is a position. They came 10, 9, 11 and 8 times, averaging 9.5, and spent £9.60, £11.20, £8.40 and £10.50, averaging £9.92. The group sits at 9.5 visits and £9.92.

That average position is the centroid, which is the technical name for the centre from the second question. The word mean is the arithmetic average, the add-them-up-and-divide operation, and a centroid is just the mean worked out separately for every feature. Two features, two means, one position.

A centroid is almost never a real member. Nobody visits 9.5 times a month. It is a summary drawn in the same space as the customers so that the same distance formula works on it, which is the entire reason for having one. That is why it gets its own mark: squares are people, crosses are averages.

The figure below draws one group and its centroid. Squares filled with colour are currently in the group and hollow ones are not. The cross is the running average of whoever is in, and the thin lines from each member to the cross are the membership lines from the legend. picture it

A mobile hanging from the ceiling with a weight for each member. The point you would hold it by to keep it level is the centroid. Move a weight and the balance point drifts toward it; hang one far out on an arm and it drifts a long way.

The centroid is not a member, it is the group's balance point, and it moves the instant membership changes.

Try selecting everybody in the figure. The cross lands at 11.75 visits and £12.08, out in empty space where nobody lives, describing no one. A centroid summarises well only when the thing it summarises really was one group, which is a quiet warning about what a bad clustering will hand you.

Now two pieces are on the table and they lock together awkwardly. Distance tells you which centroid a customer is nearest. Averaging tells you where a centroid belongs given its members. Each needs the other's answer first, which sounds like a dead end and is in fact the algorithm.

Guess, then let two moves fix your guess

The circle breaks with a move that feels like cheating: guess.

Put three centroids down anywhere at all. They will be wrong. Then repeat two moves until they stop being wrong.

The assign step gives every customer to whichever centroid is nearest, using the distance from the triangle. The update step moves every centroid to the average position of whoever just chose it. Assign, update, assign, update, and nothing else.

This is k-means. The means in the name is the averaging in the update step. The k is how many centroids you put down at the start, which here is three, and that letter is the third question wearing a disguise. It gets a chapter shortly.

The pinned figure below shows the three crosses and the twelve squares. Each square is coloured by whichever cross currently owns it, and the thin line from a square to a cross shows that ownership. Watching those lines snap to a different cross is watching the assign step happen; watching a cross slide across the chart is watching the update step.

Three guesses, two of them nearly on top of each other

The green and red crosses both start in the commuter corner, on Ana and Ben, who sit three units apart. The blue cross starts on Eli. No customer has been assigned to anything yet.

Assign: everybody joins their nearest cross

Ana and Cara take green, Ben and Dev take red. Ivo and Kit are just close enough to red to take it too. The remaining six, all four big spenders plus Jo and Lena, take blue, because blue is the only cross anywhere near the left of the chart. It is a bad grouping, and the algorithm has no way of knowing that.

Update: each cross walks to the average of whoever picked it

Red was holding Ben and Dev at one end and Ivo and Kit at the other, so its average pulls it away from the corner and out toward the middle of the chart, from 19 visits to 15. Green and blue barely shift. That one move is what starts the repair.

Assign again, and four customers switch sides

Green has moved deeper into the commuter corner, so Ben and Dev now prefer it to red. Red has moved into the middle, so Jo and Lena now prefer it to blue. Each of the three crosses is suddenly holding exactly one of the three huddles.

Update again, and the three groups separate

Green settles at 21.25 visits and £3.55, red at 9.5 and £9.92, blue at 4.5 and £22.78. Total inertia has fallen from 492.72 to 58.99, which is the tightest arrangement of three groups this data allows.

Assign once more, nothing moves, and it stops

Every customer is already with their nearest cross, so no memberships change, so the averages do not change, so the crosses do not move. That is convergence: not a target that was reached, just a round in which nothing happened. The algorithm halts because there is nothing left to do.

picture it

Three market stalls dropped at random in a town, and every resident shopping at whichever stall is closest. Each evening every stall owner looks at where his customers actually live and moves his stall to the middle of them. Overnight some residents find a different stall is now nearer and switch. The stalls move again.

Do this for a few days and it stops: nobody wants to switch, so no stall has any reason to move. Neither the stalls nor the residents ever agreed on a plan. The arrangement fell out of everybody following one small selfish rule, which is also how a constellation gets drawn.

Two properties deserve naming while they are fresh. Every round either improves the arrangement or leaves it identical, so the process cannot wander forever; on twelve customers it finishes in two or three rounds. And nothing in it ever consulted a correct answer, because there is not one. It only ever compared distances.

Which leaves the number nobody has justified. Three centroids went down because three groups were visible on the chart. Sam has three thousand customers in fifty features and can see nothing at all. Where does the three come from?

You choose k, and k changes the answer

Here is the part that surprises people who assumed the computer works out how many groups there are. It does not, and the constellation chapter already told you why: there is nothing in the data that says 3 rather than 5, in the same way there was nothing in the sky that said 48 rather than 88.

k is the number of centroids to put down, and it is an input you supply before the algorithm runs, exactly like the data itself. k-means with k set to 5 hands back five groups. It does so on data with three obvious groups, on data with one, and on pure noise, with no complaint and no warning, because finding five nearest-centroid assignments is the only thing it was ever asked to do.

In the figure below the only thing changing is how many crosses get put down. Every square takes the colour of its nearest cross, the lines show the ownership, and the list underneath prints who ended up with whom, so you can read the actual groups rather than guessing from the colours.

Drag k from 1 to 6. Every value returns a complete, converged, perfectly valid grouping. Nothing on the chart flags k = 5 as a worse description of this bakery than k = 3.

Watch k = 4, which is the interesting one. It keeps the commuters and the middle huddle intact and splits the four big spenders down the middle: Eli and Gus in one group, Fay and Hana in another. That split is not nonsense. Hana really does spend seven pounds more per visit than Gus. It is simply a finer cut than Sam has any use for, which is the Greeks splitting an arc into two because the halves rise at different times.

And k = 1 is the honest edge case: one centroid, everybody in it, the average customer of the bakery, a real and completely useless summary.

picture it

Handing someone a bag of pebbles and telling them to make five piles. They will make five piles. They will not come back and say the pebbles really wanted to be in three, because you asked for five and five is what a helpful person delivers.

k is a question you ask, not a question the algorithm answers. Everything from here is about how to ask a good one.

So there must be some way to compare the k = 3 answer with the k = 4 answer and say which describes the bakery better. Since there is no answer key, that comparison has to be built out of the data itself. The first attempt is the number that has been sitting quietly in the corner of every figure so far.

Inertia measures tightness, and it always falls

A tight group is one whose members sit close to their own centroid, which is the same as saying the thin lines in the figures are short. That sentence becomes a single number by the following recipe.

Take one customer. Measure the distance to their own centroid. Square it. Do that for all twelve and add the twelve squared distances together. That total is inertia, also written as the within-cluster sum of squares, which is the same instruction read aloud: within each cluster, sum the squares.

Small inertia means tight groups. At k = 3 the inertia of this bakery is 58.99. So run every k, take the lowest, done. Except that never works, and the reason is worth seeing rather than being told.

The figure below plots inertia up the side against k along the bottom. Each dot is one value of k. When you select a k, a shaded bar is drawn spanning the vertical gap between the previous k and this one, so the height of that bar is exactly the improvement that adding one more group bought you.

Figure 11 · inertia at every kk = 3 · inertia 58.99
Click a k to see the drop it bought. The vertical bar shows how much inertia fell coming from the previous k. Two enormous bars, then a run of small ones.

Inertia falls at every single k without exception, and that is not a quirk of this bakery but a guarantee. Adding a centroid can only ever give customers a closer cross to be measured against, never a further one. Push it to the limit: at k = 12 every customer is their own centroid, every distance is zero, and inertia is exactly 0. A perfect score, and a grouping that tells Sam nothing whatsoever.

picture it

Judging coat pegs by how far anyone has to walk to reach one. Install more pegs and the walk gets shorter. Always. Install one peg per person and nobody walks at all.

That is a real measurement and a useless decision, because the score rewards the very thing you were trying to ration. What you actually want to notice is the peg that stopped saving anybody a walk.

So the thing to read is not the inertia but the size of each drop. One group to two cuts it from 1417.65 to 416.40. Two to three cuts it again to 58.99, a fall of 86 percent. Three to four saves only 27.5 more. The first two additions bought something real; the third bought detail.

Plotted against k, that pattern plunges and then flattens, with a visible corner where the plunging stops. The corner is the elbow, and reading it is the oldest way of choosing k. Here it sits squarely at 3.

The elbow is a judgement, not a calculation. This bakery has an unusually obvious one because its three groups were built far apart. Real data routinely gives a smooth curve with no corner at all, and two analysts will read the same plot as 4 and as 6. The elbow narrows the field; it rarely settles the argument.

What would help is a score that can get worse when k goes up, so that a peak means something. Inertia can never do that. Something else has to.

Silhouette asks each customer whether they are in the right group

Put yourself in Gus's position, at 6 visits and £19.20, grouped with Eli, Fay and Hana.

Two questions decide whether that grouping does Gus any favours. How far is he, on average, from the other members of his own group? Call that a. And how far would he be, on average, from the members of the next nearest group, the one he would join if his own vanished? Call that b.

If b is much bigger than a, Gus is comfortably home. If a and b are about equal he is sitting on a border and the grouping says nothing much about him. If a is bigger than b, he is closer to the neighbours than to his own people, and he has been filed wrong.

The silhouette of a customer packages those two into one score.

silhouette = ( b − a ) ÷ the larger of a and b

Gus: a = 8.71   b = 16.75   →  (16.75 − 8.71) ÷ 16.75 = 0.48
Ivo: a = 1.91   b = 13.00   →  (13.00 − 1.91) ÷ 13.00 = 0.85

Dividing by the larger of the two pins the score between −1 and +1 whatever the units are. Near +1 means well inside the right group, near 0 means on a border, below 0 means wrongly assigned. The score for a whole grouping is the average across everybody, and unlike inertia it is free to go down.

The figure below draws one horizontal bar per customer, sorted into their groups. Bar length is that customer's silhouette score, so long bars are people the grouping describes well and short ones are people sitting on a border. Bar colour is the group they were put in. The heavy vertical line is the average across everybody, which is the single number usually quoted.

Now click k = 4, which cut the big spenders in two, and watch why the average falls to 0.682. Fay drops from 0.769 to 0.341 and Eli from 0.719 to 0.372. Splitting four customers who belonged together left each pair with a single groupmate to be near while the other pair sat right next door as the nearest alternative. Both halves are now borderline and the score says so.

picture it

At a party, being asked how well you fit the group you are standing in. You compare how near your own circle is against how near the next circle over is. In the middle of your friends with strangers across the room scores high. Standing between two circles, half in each conversation, scores near zero.

Split a real group in half and everybody in it starts standing on a border. That is why this score can fall where inertia cannot: cutting a group tightens it and simultaneously manufactures a very close neighbour.

Across k = 2 to 6 the averages run 0.597, 0.753, 0.682, 0.597, 0.500. There is a peak, it is at 3, and it agrees with the elbow. When two independent scores agree, that is about as much confidence as this kind of work offers.

Use both, and use your eyes. Inertia is cheap on huge datasets and tells you when a k is buying nothing. Silhouette costs far more, because it needs the distance between every pair of rows, but it can be wrong in the useful direction. Neither knows what Sam intends to do. If she can only run two promotions, k = 2 is the right answer whatever the scores say.

Both scores were computed on one particular run. That turns out to assume more than it sounds, because running it again does not always give the same run.

Run it twice and you can get two different answers

The algorithm began by guessing, and nothing has yet been said about guessing badly.

Try this specific bad guess: put the three starting centroids on Ana, Ben and Cara, who all sit within three units of each other in the commuter corner. The two moves then run exactly as before, and the whole thing converges normally, with no error and no warning.

Press "run 100 random starts". The tally counts how many of them landed on the best arrangement and how many got stuck somewhere worse. Both outcomes are converged; only one is good.

An arrangement that cannot be improved by any single switch but is not the best available is a local optimum. Local because it is the best thing in its immediate neighbourhood and every small move away makes things worse. k-means cannot see past that neighbourhood; it only ever compares a customer to the crosses currently on the board.

picture it

Walking downhill in fog, one step at a time, always taking the steepest step down, stopping when every direction is up. That rule reliably gets you to the bottom of something. Whether it is the bottom of the valley or the bottom of a puddle on the hillside depends entirely on where you were standing when the fog came down.

The algorithm cannot tell a puddle from a valley, because from inside either one every direction is up.

Measured over twenty thousand runs on this data, a start that picks three customers uniformly at random reaches the best arrangement 73.0 percent of the time. Roughly one run in four lands somewhere worse, and on messier data with more groups that fraction gets considerably worse.

Two fixes are standard and every real implementation uses both. The first is brute force: run it ten times from ten different random starts and keep whichever ended with the lowest inertia. That is what the n_init setting does in the common libraries, and it is why they run the algorithm ten times when you thought you asked for one.

The second improves the guessing itself. k-means++ picks the first centroid at random, then picks each remaining one with a probability weighted by how far a customer sits from the nearest centroid already chosen, so unclaimed territory is strongly favoured and the starting centroids spread out instead of huddling. On this data it reaches the best arrangement 96.7 percent of the time against 73.0 for a plain random start. It has been the default in scikit-learn for years, which is why most people never learn this problem exists.

Neither fix is a guarantee. Ten good starts can all land in the same puddle. What they buy is that failure becomes rare rather than routine. If your groupings vary between runs, that is not a bug to be silenced with a fixed random seed; it is the data telling you the groups are not well separated.

Everything so far has assumed the features being measured are a fair basis for comparison. That assumption has been quietly load-bearing since the triangle, and it is not safe.

Two quiet ways it hands you a confident wrong answer

Change nothing about the bakery. Just record spend in pence instead of pounds.

Ana's £3.40 becomes 340. Hana's £26.40 becomes 2640. Every customer is the same customer, the shop is the same shop, and the distance formula is about to behave completely differently, because it squares whatever it is handed. A gap of 2300 pence contributes over five million to a squared distance; a gap of 19 visits contributes 361. Visits has not been removed from the calculation. It has been outvoted roughly fourteen thousand to one.

This is the first quiet failure, and it follows from the formula rather than being a flaw in it. The feature with the widest spread of numbers gets the loudest vote, and the spread depends on what unit somebody happened to write it in.

The figure below draws one horizontal bar per feature. Bar length is the share of all the distance between customers that this feature is responsible for, added across all sixty-six pairs. The dashed vertical marker sits where an equal say would be, so a bar reaching well past it is a feature that is outvoting the others.

As recorded, spend carries 56.5 percent of the total distance and visits 43.5, which is roughly balanced by luck. In pence, spend carries essentially all of it. Bring in the third feature raw and pastry share, running from 22 to 61, takes 52.2 percent on its own, more than the other two together.

The standard fix is standardising: for each feature, subtract that feature's mean and divide by its standard deviation, which is the usual measure of how spread out a set of values is. Every feature then comes out centred on zero and spread by one, so a customer who is unusually high on visits and one who is unusually high on spend are unusual by comparable amounts. The bars land on 33.3 percent each. The general name for this family of adjustments is scaling.

picture it

Judging a decathlete by adding up their raw numbers: seconds in the sprint, metres in the javelin, centimetres in the high jump. The javelin is around 60 and the sprint around 10, so the javelin decides the medal and the sprint is decoration. Real scoring tables convert every event to points first, so a good javelin and a good sprint are worth the same.

Standardising is the scoring table. Without it, the event with the biggest numbers picks the winner.

This bakery survives the unit change, and it is worth saying why rather than letting the problem look theoretical. Its three groups differ so sharply on both features at once that no reweighting can pull them apart. That is luck. The moment two features disagree about who belongs together, the louder one wins, and loudness was set by a unit somebody chose.

The second failure is not about units at all. It is about what shape a group is allowed to be.

k‑means does not find groups. It finds the best three, or two, centroids, and the groups are whatever falls out.

If a shape this obvious defeats it, what else is out there?

Three ways to cluster, running on the same points

k-means is the one everybody meets first because it is fast, easy to explain, and works well when groups really are roundish blobs. It is one family among several, and the others start from different answers to the three questions.

Hierarchical clustering never guesses. It starts with every point as its own group, finds the two closest groups, merges them, and repeats until one group is left. That produces a full merge history, drawn as a branching diagram called a dendrogram, where the height of each join is how far apart the two merged groups were. You do not pick k in advance; you cut the tree at whatever height you like afterwards, and where you cut decides how many groups you get. This is much closer to what the constellation-drawers actually did: join the two nearest, then the next two, and stop when the shape looks like something.

The measure of how far apart two groups are is the choice that matters, and it has a name: linkage. Single linkage uses the distance between the two nearest members, which lets a group chain along a thin arc one point at a time. Average linkage uses the average over all pairs, which prefers compact blobs and fails on those same arcs.

DBSCAN throws out the idea of a group centre altogether and works from density, meaning how many points sit within a short distance of each other. You give it a radius and a count of how many neighbours a point needs inside that radius to count as being in a crowded region. Crowded points within reach of each other form a group, the group grows outward for as long as the crowding continues, and anything left in a sparse area is labelled noise and put in no group at all. It finds however many groups exist, including none, and it is the only one of the three allowed to say a point belongs nowhere.

When a grouping is run, points take the colour of their group. Two crescents are about the most obvious shape a person can see and about the hardest thing for k-means to find.

picture it

Three people sorting the same pile of buttons. The first sets out three saucers and puts every button in the nearest saucer. The second glues the two most similar buttons together, then the next two, until there is one lump, leaving a record you can cut anywhere. The third looks for places where the buttons lie thick on the table, calls each thick patch a group, and sweeps the strays aside.

None of them is sorting wrongly. They disagree because "group" meant a saucer, a merge history and a thick patch, and those are three different things. Which is the constellation disagreement again, now happening inside a computer.

A fourth family is worth knowing by name even though it is not drawn here. A Gaussian mixture model runs almost the same two-step loop as k-means, but each group carries a width and an orientation as well as a centre, and every point gets a probability of belonging to each group rather than a hard assignment. That lets groups be stretched ellipses and lets a borderline point be reported as sixty percent one thing and forty percent the other, which k-means can never say.

A rough guide. Roundish groups, many rows, needs to be fast: k-means. Want the structure at every level of granularity, or have very few rows: hierarchical. Odd shapes, unknown number of groups, real outliers you want excluded: DBSCAN. Overlapping groups where you want a probability rather than a verdict: a Gaussian mixture. All of them still need you to decide what counts as alike, so all of them still face the scaling decision.

That leaves the question that should come before any of this: should Sam be clustering at all?

Reach for clustering when you cannot name the groups yet

The honest test is a question about your own situation rather than about the data, and the sky answers it. Were the constellations available anywhere to be looked up? No. Was there any way to get them except by looking at what sat near what? No. That is the condition.

If you can already write down the categories you want and you have examples of each, you do not want clustering. You want the supervised version and it will do better, because it gets to use the answers you already have. Clustering earns its place at exactly one moment: you suspect there is structure, you cannot name it, and you want a first draft of its shape.

picture it

Two questions about a room full of strangers. "Sort these people into the six departments" has an answer somebody can check, and whoever knows it should just tell you. "Are there natural cliques in this room, and what are they?" has no answer waiting anywhere, and looking at who stands near whom is the only way in.

Clustering answers the second kind of question and quietly fails the first, because it will happily return six groups that are not your six departments and look just as convincing.

Three habits keep this work honest, and all three fall out of the page.

Scale your features before measuring anything, or accept that the feature with the biggest numbers is making the decision. Run it several times, and if the groups move between runs, believe the instability rather than the last run you happened to look at. And treat the result as a hypothesis rather than a finding: give the groups provisional names, read the actual rows inside each one, and see whether somebody who knows the business recognises them.

That last habit is the constellation test, and it is the one people skip. Sam should look at her four commuters and ask whether "people buying breakfast on the way to work" describes them. If it does, the grouping earned its keep and she has drawn a constellation somebody can navigate by. If it does not, the numbers have arranged themselves into something real that is nevertheless of no use to anybody, which happens more often than anyone admits.

Clustering is the constellation instinct, made explicit enough to run. Turn each row into a position so that alike becomes a distance. Summarise a group by its average. Guess some centres, hand everything to its nearest one, move each centre to the middle of what it caught, and repeat until nothing moves. Everything difficult comes afterwards: how many groups, measured in which units, and whether the shapes you drew mean anything to the person who asked.

Into the weeds

Why squared distance, and not plain distance?

Because the centroid, the plain arithmetic mean, is exactly the point that minimises the sum of squared distances to a set of points. Change the objective to unsquared distance and the best centre becomes the geometric median instead, which has no simple formula and has to be solved iteratively. The whole reason the update step can be "take the average" in one line is that the thing being minimised is squared. The algorithm that does the unsquared version, using actual data points as centres, is called k‑medoids, and it is far more resistant to outliers and far slower.

Does k‑means always stop?

Yes, and the argument is short. Each round either changes at least one assignment or changes none. Any assignment change strictly lowers inertia, the update step also lowers or holds it, and there are finitely many ways to split twelve rows into three groups. A quantity that strictly decreases over a finite set of states cannot cycle, so the process runs out of states and halts. On real data it usually takes a handful of rounds, though contrived datasets exist that take exponentially many.

What if a centroid ends up with no customers?

It happens, most often when k is large or a start was unlucky. The average of an empty set is undefined, so implementations have to do something: scikit-learn relocates the orphaned centroid to the point currently furthest from any centroid and carries on. Other libraries drop it and return fewer groups than you asked for. This is worth knowing because it means a request for k = 8 can quietly return 7.

Standardising versus min-max scaling

Standardising subtracts the mean and divides by the standard deviation, giving every column mean 0 and spread 1, and it leaves outliers sitting far out where they are. Min-max scaling squeezes each column into the range 0 to 1 by subtracting the minimum and dividing by the range, which puts every column on identical footing but lets a single extreme value compress everything else into a tiny band. For distance work, standardising is the usual default. When outliers are severe, a version built on the median and the interquartile range, both of which ignore extremes, beats either.

The curse of dimensionality

With three hundred columns rather than two, something unhelpful happens to distance: the ratio between the nearest and furthest neighbour of a point drifts toward 1, so everything is roughly equally far from everything else and "nearest centroid" stops carrying information. This is why clustering on high dimensional data is usually preceded by a dimension reduction step such as principal component analysis, or by picking a smaller set of columns on purpose.

How silhouette handles a group of one

It cannot be computed, because a lone member has no other members to average a distance against. The convention is to score such a point 0, treating it as neither well placed nor badly placed. This matters when comparing values of k, because a k that strands single points gets an artificially neutral contribution from them rather than a penalty, which flatters it slightly.

Other ways to choose k

The gap statistic compares your inertia curve against the curve you would get from uniformly random data with the same bounding box, and picks the k where the gap between them is widest, which gives a principled way of saying "no groups at all". The Calinski-Harabasz and Davies-Bouldin indices both compare between-group spread against within-group spread and are much cheaper than silhouette. None of them resolves the fundamental problem, which is that the number of groups in a dataset is often not a well defined quantity.

Mini-batch k‑means

The assign step measures every row against every centroid, so a full pass costs rows times k times columns. On tens of millions of rows that is slow. Mini-batch k‑means samples a few thousand rows per round and nudges the centroids using only those, trading a small loss in final inertia for an enormous speedup. It is the standard choice at large scale and is available as a drop-in in scikit-learn.

Clustering things that are not numbers

Everything here assumed rows of numbers with a meaningful subtraction. Categorical columns break that: the difference between "Tuesday" and "Friday" is not 3. Options include one-hot encoding then treating the result as numeric (workable but it inflates the column count and makes every category equidistant), k‑modes which replaces the mean with the most common value, or Gower distance which defines a per-column similarity for mixed data and averages them. For text, the usual route is to convert documents to embedding vectors first and cluster those.
