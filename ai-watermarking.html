What a watermark actually is

A watermark in paper is not printed on the sheet. It is a change in the thickness of the sheet itself, made while the paper is still wet. You handle the note every day and never see it; hold it to a window and it is unmistakable. You cannot peel it off, because there is no layer to peel.

Text watermarking borrows that idea exactly. The mark does not live in a header, a metadata field, or an invisible character that a copy-paste would strip. It lives in which words got chosen — a bias applied at the moment the model picks each one, keyed to a secret only the model's owner holds.

The mechanism, in two pictures

Every word a model writes is a draw from a bag of weighted options, not a decision. That is the opening the watermark uses. Before each draw, the words already written are hashed together with a secret key to produce a number, and that number cuts the vocabulary into two lists: a favoured half and an unfavoured half. Nothing about a word's meaning decides which side it lands on, and the split changes at every position.

The model then adds a little weight to everything on the favoured list, rescales, and draws as normal. Any single word proves nothing — a favoured word could always have been chosen by chance. Over three hundred words, chance stops being a plausible explanation, and that is the whole game: the expected count grows with the length of the passage while the wobble around it grows only with the square root.

What the book covers

  • Why detecting AI text by its style does not work. Style classifiers fail in exactly the case that matters, and the failure is structural rather than a matter of more training data.
  • The green-list / red-list scheme, worked by hand. Hashing the context with a key, splitting the vocabulary, biasing the logits, and drawing — with the actual arithmetic at every step, not a block diagram.
  • The strength dial, and what it costs. One number decides how hard the thumb presses on the scale. Press harder and detection gets easier while the writing gets worse; the trade is the design decision.
  • Detection as a statistical claim. How a hit count becomes a z-score, why three hundred words are worth far more than sixty, and what a detector is entitled to say.
  • The attacks, honestly. Light editing, paraphrase, short passages and low-entropy text — which of them the watermark survives, and which of them end the argument.
  • What is already deployed. Where text watermarking is running in production today, and the scale of it, which surprises most people.

Written for engineers, product people and anyone who has been asked whether a piece of text was machine-written and wants to answer with something better than a hunch. No prior background in cryptography or statistics is assumed — the book builds both as it goes.
